# Must One Submit to a Null Sentence? A Response to Christian B. Wagner - v0.1

## 1. Wagner's Argument

Wagner analyzes the three documents that make up Rome's response to the consecrations of July 1, 2026: the decree of the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith declaring the excommunication of the six bishops, the attached Explanatory Note extending the judgment of schism to the Society's clergy and formally adhering laity, and the 1996 Explanatory Note of the Pontifical Council for Legislative Texts, which the DDF "makes its own."[1]

His central move is the distinction between an excommunication that is *invalid* and one that is merely *unjust*. On the part of the decree, an excommunication can be unjust either because the lawful form was not observed, in which case it is void, or because it lacks a just cause, in which case it is valid and takes effect. He concludes:

> "This bears repeating: excommunications done in the legal form, yet without just cause, are not without effect. This is the common teaching of theologians and is the teaching of St. Thomas."[2]

He then quotes St. Thomas (ST Suppl. q. 21 a. 4), infers that "those who are unjustly excommunicated sin mortally by contemning their sentence and are truly removed from membership in the Church," and dispatches the SSPX's canonical defense in a single sentence:

> "While some will cite mitigating factors which they argue will render the excommunications null (namely, the provision of can. 1323, 4°), this was rejected by the aforementioned document from the Pontifical Council for Legislative Texts."[2]

I will argue three things. First, Wagner's Thomas quotation cannot carry this weight, because Thomas himself distinguishes the case where the sentence is *void*, and a void sentence binds no one. Second, the SSPX's argument is precisely a nullity argument, and it rests on provisions of the Code that neither Wagner nor the 1996 Note ever engages: can. 1323 n. 7 and can. 1324 §1 n. 8 with §3. Third, the 1996 Note's requirement that a state of necessity "must objectively occur" is one the SSPX affirms rather than denies; and once "objectively" is read correctly, the whole canonical defense goes through, leaving the Roman position only two escape routes, which I will examine on their merits.

## 2. What St. Thomas Actually Says

Here is the passage Wagner quotes, with emphasis added:

> "Secondly, on the part of the excommunication, through there being no proper cause, or through the sentence being passed without the forms of law being observed. In this case, **if the error, on the part of the sentence, be such as to render the sentence void, this has no effect, for there is no excommunication**; but if the error does not annul the sentence, this takes effect, and the person excommunicated should humbly submit (which will be credited to him as a merit), and either seek absolution from the person who has excommunicated him, **or appeal to a higher judge**." (ST Suppl. q. 21 a. 4)[3]

Thomas's counsel of humble submission applies to the valid but unjust sentence. Of the void sentence he says the opposite: it has no effect, for there is no excommunication. One need not submit to a null sentence; there is nothing to submit to. And even for the valid but unjust sentence, Thomas names appeal to a higher judge as a legitimate response alongside submission.

Now, which case does the SSPX claim? Not the second. The Society does not say: "we are validly excommunicated, but unjustly, and therefore we ignore it." That position would indeed fall under Thomas's condemnation. It claims the third case, nullity, as its Superior General states in his letter to Pope Leo XIV of July 3, calling the sanctions "objectively unjust **and invalid**."[4]

This matters because of the form Rome chose. The 2026 decree, like Cardinal Gantin's decree of 1988, is declaratory:

> "I therefore declare for all juridical purposes that both the aforementioned Bishop Alfonso de Galarreta and Pascal Schreiber, Michael Goldade, Michel Poinsinet de Sivry, and Marc Hanappier **have incurred ipso facto** the latae sententiae excommunication reserved to the Apostolic See."[1]

A latae sententiae penalty is incurred, if at all, by the act itself, under the conditions the law itself establishes. A declaratory decree establishes a fact; it does not create it. If the exempting provisions of the Code applied, there was nothing to declare, and the sentence is void in Thomas's sense, not unjust in his second sense. (This argument concerns only the latae sententiae form Rome actually chose. A ferendae sententiae penalty imposed through a proper penal process would be valid though unjust, and I concede that case would fall under Thomas's second category; see my defense, §6.4.2.[5])

So Thomas's text cannot settle the dispute, because the dispute is over which of his cases applies: was the penalty incurred? Everything turns on that question, and quoting Thomas on submission against a party whose entire claim is nullity simply begs it.

## 3. Rome Has Declared Such a Decree Null Before: The Hawaii Six

That a declaratory decree of latae sententiae excommunication for schism can be null is not a theoretical construction. Rome itself has said so, in a case concerning precisely the SSPX.

On May 1, 1991, Bishop Joseph Ferrario of Honolulu declared six lay faithful excommunicated for schism under can. 1364 §1. Their offense: they attended the SSPX's Our Lady of Fatima Chapel in Honolulu and procured the services of Bishop Williamson, one of the four bishops "excommunicated" in 1988, to administer confirmations there.[6] The six did exactly what Thomas recommends: they appealed to a higher judge. In 1993 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, under Cardinal Ratzinger, ruled on the case of Mrs. Patricia Morley, one of the six:

> "This Congregation has examined carefully all the available documentation and has ascertained that the activities engaged in by the Petitioner, though blameworthy on various accounts, are not sufficient to constitute the crime of schism. Since Mrs. Morley did not, in fact, commit the crime of schism and thus did not incur the 'latae sententiae' penalty, it is clear that the Decree of the Bishop lacks the precondition on which is founded. This Congregation, noting all of the above, is obliged to declare null and void the aforesaid Decree of the Ordinary of Honolulu."[7]

The letter communicated through the Apostolic Pro-Nuncio put it this way:

> "From the examination of the case, conducted on the basis of the Law of the Church, it did not result that the facts referred to in the above-mentioned decree are formal schismatic acts in the strict sense, as they do not constitute the offense of schism; and therefore the Congregation holds that the Decree of May 1, 1991 lacks foundation and hence validity."[6]

Three lessons. First, the exact structure the SSPX alleges in 2026 has already occurred: a bishop declared, in due legal form, that persons "incurred ipso facto" the excommunication for schism, and the Holy See later established that the penalty had never been incurred and the decree was therefore null. Declaratory decrees can lack validity, not merely justice. Second, the judgment that association with the SSPX constitutes schism has been made by legitimate authority before, and it was wrong by Rome's own account: acts "blameworthy on various accounts" were nevertheless "not sufficient to constitute the crime of schism." Third, an obvious difference must be conceded: in 1991 the decree came from a local bishop and a higher tribunal existed; in 2026 the decree comes from the DDF itself. A legal remedy nonetheless exists: the decree bears no papal approval _in forma specifica_ that would make it unappealable, and on July 11, 2026 the Society submitted to the Dicastery the request for revocation under cann. 1734 ff., the mandatory preliminary step to a hierarchical recourse.[8] In addition there is the letter to the pope.[4] But nullity does not depend on a tribunal declaring it. A null decree is null even if no one competent ever says so; Ferrario's decree was null from May 1, 1991, not from 1993. What the absence of a higher tribunal changes is not the fact of nullity but the peril of claiming it for oneself, a point I return to in section 5.

## 4. The Canonical Defense That Was Not Engaged

Wagner mentions only can. 1323 n. 4, the state of necessity itself. The defense has never rested on n. 4 alone. The current Code (Book VI as revised in 2021, in which can. 1323 and can. 1324 §1 remained verbatim unchanged and can. 1324 §3 was only supplemented by the sentence that "attamen" milder penalties or penances may be imposed on the offender, which presupposes the exemption from the latae sententiae penalty[9]) provides:[10]

> "Can. 1323: No one is liable to a penalty who, when violating a law or precept: ... 4° acted under the compulsion of grave fear, even if only relative, or by reason of necessity or grave inconvenience, unless, however, the act is intrinsically evil or tends to be harmful to souls; ... 7° thought, through no personal fault, that some one of the circumstances existed which are mentioned in nn. 4 or 5."

> "Can. 1324 §1: The perpetrator of a violation is not exempted from penalty, but the penalty prescribed in the law or precept must be diminished, or a penance substituted in its place, if the offence was committed by: ... 5° one who was compelled by grave fear, even if only relative, or who acted by reason of necessity or grave inconvenience, if the offence is intrinsically evil or tends to be harmful to souls; ... 8° one who erroneously, but culpably, thought that some one of the circumstances existed which are mentioned in can. 1323 nn. 4 or 5. ...
> §3: In the circumstances mentioned in § 1, the offender is not bound by a latae sententiae penalty, but may have lesser penalties or penances imposed for the purposes of repentance or repair of scandal."

The structure of the defense must be stated precisely, because it is easy to misstate. The primary claim is that the state of necessity **really obtains** (can. 1323 n. 4): the Modernist crisis diagnosed since Pius X is a fact about the Church, not a feeling about her (my defense, §2 and §6.1.2[5]). The error provisions enter as a subsidiary layer, *arguendo*: their function is to show that the factual dispute with Rome does not even need to be settled for the penal question. For suppose a judge sides with Rome on the facts. Then the Society did not lie about the necessity; it *erred* about it. And the legislator has provided for exactly that case, twice: an error without fault exempts from the penalty entirely (1323 n. 7), and an error *even with fault* excludes the latae sententiae penalty (1324 §1 n. 8 with §3). On every branch, the automatic excommunication fails; and with it fails the declaratory decree, which declares a penalty that on no branch was incurred.

That these norms apply precisely to episcopal consecration without mandate has been expressly stated by Rome's own legislative authority. In 2011 the Pontifical Council for Legislative Texts declared regarding can. 1382 (now can. 1387) that the circumstances of can. 1324 §1 n. 5 are ones "che la storia ha dimostrato compatibili con delitti di questa natura"; they must be examined for each participant individually, and "ciascuno di loro conosce in cuor suo il grado del personale coinvolgimento e la retta coscienza indicherà a ognuno se è incorso in una pena latae sententiae".[11] The Dicastery's current handbook on penal law likewise states that the delict can be punished "ferendae sententiae", "particularly in the cases foreseen by can. 1324 §3".[12] Rome thus has a regular path if it considers the latae sententiae penalty excluded; it did not choose it.

On the basis of this argument, the canonist Fr. Gerald Murray concluded in his licentiate thesis that Lefebvre was, "canonically speaking … not guilty of a schismatic act punishable by canon law."[13] Murray partially withdrew his interpretation of can. 1324 in 1996, saying it had been too broad, since the error must concern something real rather than something merely imagined, and in 2026 he publicly defended the opposite position.[14] I therefore do not cite him as an authority. His later objection, however, deserves an answer: if n. 8 covered only an error about a state of necessity that actually exists, there would be no error at all but the case of n. 4, and n. 8 would have no scope of its own. The objection holds only in the narrower form that the error must rest on real facts; here it does, as section 5 shows. It is worth quoting what the 1996 Note, Wagner's authority against the mitigating factors, says about Murray's thesis:

> "One cannot furnish any judgement on the argumentation of Murray's thesis... because it is not known, and the two articles which refer to it appear confused. However, doubt cannot reasonably be cast upon the validity of the excommunication of the Bishops declared in the Motu Proprio and the Decree."[15]

The Note declines to examine the strongest form of the nullity argument and then asserts that its conclusion cannot reasonably be doubted. Neither the Note nor Wagner's article ever discusses can. 1323 n. 7, can. 1324 §1 n. 8, or can. 1324 §3. Its status must be added: the Council's covering letter expressly states that the request did not call for an authentic interpretation, since there was no _dubium iuris_, and its core statement on exempting circumstances is only modal ("non sembra che si possa trovare … qualche circostanza esimente o attenuante"). The same document also states that the provisions of Book VI, "anche i cann. 1323–1324", are to be applied to the delict of schism.[16]

## 5. "Such a State Must Objectively Occur"

The substantive core of the 1996 Note is this passage:

> **Original:** "Quanto allo stato di necessità in cui Mons. Lefebvre pensasse di trovarsi, va tenuto presente che tale stato deve verificarsi oggettivamente, e che non si dà mai una necessità di ordinare Vescovi contro la volontà del Romano Pontefice, Capo del Collegio dei Vescovi."[16]
>
> **Translation:** "As far as the state of necessity in which Mons. Lefebvre thought to find himself, one must keep before one that such a state must occur objectively, and there is never a necessity to ordain Bishops contrary to the will of the Roman Pontiff, Head of the College of Bishops."[15]

Two distinct claims: (a) the state of necessity must objectively occur; (b) a necessity of this kind can never exist. Claim (b) is a category claim and I treat it in section 7.2. Here I take claim (a), and the first thing to notice is that **the SSPX affirms it**. The Society has never argued that its sincere conviction constitutes the necessity. It argues that the necessity objectively obtains, which is why Fr. Pagliarani's letter calls the sanctions "**objectively** unjust and invalid."[4] The disagreement between Rome and Écône is not about whether necessity must be objective. It is about whether it objectively obtains: a disagreement about a fact.

That makes the status of Rome's contrary finding decisive. "There is no state of necessity" is not an exercise of the infallible magisterium; it is a factual assessment embedded in disciplinary acts, and it meets none of the conditions of *Pastor Aeternus* (my defense, §6.1.2[5]). Let me be clear about what this does not license. A judgment of the supreme authority about the condition of the Church is not something anyone may brush aside. The presumption stands with the authority, and it takes reasons of the gravest kind, held with qualified competence and acted on with proportionality, to overcome it (my defense sets out these threshold conditions, §6.1.2[5]). But circumstances grave enough to overcome that presumption are exactly what a state of necessity *is*. The claim that such reversals of the presumption are possible at all is not a novelty: the Church has retroactively vindicated judgments of exactly this kind made from below, against the standing assessment of the competent hierarchy, in the cases of Athanasius and of Eusebius of Dorylaeum (my defense, §6.1.2[5]). And within living memory, in the Hawaii case, the authority's own determination that certain SSPX-related acts were schismatic was examined by a higher organ of the same authority and found to be wrong.

## 6. What Does "Objectively" Mean?

The word deserves scrutiny, because on a lazy reading it does no work: everyone who acts by reason of necessity believes the necessity is objective, so "it must be objective" would restate the question rather than answer it. Two readings are possible.

**Reading 1: "objectively" means "actually obtaining in reality, not merely in the agent's estimation."** I hold this reading to be correct, and it is not redundant, because of which canon it glosses. The Code itself distinguishes the circumstance (n. 4: the necessity exists) from the belief in it (n. 7: it does not exist, but the offender blamelessly thought it did). The Note's sentence is a correct gloss on n. 4: for the exempting circumstance itself, conviction is not enough. The Italian confirms it: *deve verificarsi oggettivamente*, and *verificarsi* means "to occur," "to obtain," not "to be verified by someone." But a gloss on n. 4 says nothing about n. 7 and n. 8, which are *about* belief, and which the legislator wrote precisely for the case where the objective circumstance is absent.

**Reading 2: "objectively" means "recognizable as such by the competent authority."** On this reading, once Rome has determined before the act that no necessity exists, the offender could no longer claim even erroneous belief: he would believe not against ignorance but against a judgment. I take this reading seriously because, unlike Reading 1, it would actually defeat the defense rather than talk past it. But it fails three times over. Linguistically, *verificarsi* will not bear it; the Note says the state must occur, not that it must be certified. Systematically, it proves too much: the Code grades the offender's relation to a non-existent necessity on a scale of culpability, from blameless error (n. 7) through culpable error (n. 8), and a prior pronouncement of authority appears nowhere in this scheme as a switch that turns error into non-error. What such a pronouncement really does is move the offender along the culpability scale, and the legislator provided for the far end of that scale in n. 8 and still withheld the automatic penalty. Reading 2 would make n. 8 a dead letter in exactly the cases it was written for. And structurally, it makes the authority judge in its own cause precisely where the alleged necessity consists in the failure of that authority; the Hawaii case is a standing reminder that authoritative determinations of schism in this very area have been made, examined, and reversed.

The conclusion I draw is the one Wagner's article never confronts: if "objectively" means what it says, corresponding to reality, then the entire canonical defense goes through. The objectivity requirement is affirmed, not violated; the factual dispute is real but penalty-irrelevant, because error on either grade excludes the latae sententiae penalty; and the declaratory decree of 2026 declares a penalty that was not incurred on any branch. The Roman position then has exactly two escape routes left, and the whole weight of the decree rests on them.

## 7. The Two Escape Routes

### 7.1. Can. 1325: crass, supine, or affected ignorance

> "Can. 1325: Ignorance which is crass or supine or affected can never be taken into account when applying the provisions of cann. 1323 and 1324."[10]

This, not the word "objectively," is where the serious version of the Roman argument lives. The claim would have to be: the Society's belief in a state of necessity, maintained against the declaration of the Prefect of the Dicastery of May 13, 2026[17] and the personal letter of Pope Leo XIV of June 29 ("vi prego e vi chiedo con tutto il cuore: tornate sui vostri passi!"[18]), is not error at all but affected ignorance, ignorance deliberately maintained; or crass and supine ignorance, born of neglect.

I do not think either label fits, though I hold this with less certainty than anything else in this essay. Crass or supine ignorance is the ignorance of one who never bothered to examine the question. The SSPX has examined the question of the state of necessity for fifty years, in canonical studies, formal declarations, and direct exchanges with Rome; whatever this is, it is not neglect. Affected ignorance is cultivated in order to sin more freely, the ignorance of a man who avoids finding out whether his act is forbidden. It fits badly a Society that publicly acknowledged the prohibition and the warnings and argued against their applicability with its reasons on the table. A studied error, even an obstinate one, is an error, not a cultivated ignorance; and can. 1324 §1 n. 8 is written precisely for studied errors, since blameless error already falls under 1323 n. 7. If n. 8 does not cover the offender who examined the question and got it wrong, it covers no one.

A second way to press this side deserves naming: one could argue the Society's error is not an error of *fact* (about the condition of the Church; on the facts both sides largely agree) but of *law*, namely the belief that facts of this kind can ever constitute a canonical state of necessity, when the 1996 Note says they never can. Recast this way, n. 8 would arguably not apply. My answer: the judgment "the Church is in a state of necessity" is not a legal opinion about the Code but an evaluative judgment about the concrete condition of the Church, which is exactly the kind of circumstance n. 4 names. The properly legal question, whether necessity of this kind is excluded in principle, is the Note's claim (b), which must stand on its own merits. To it now.

### 7.2. "There is never a necessity": the intrinsic-evil clause

The claim that there can never be a necessity to consecrate bishops against the will of the pope is not a finding about 1988 or 2026; it is a category claim, and its only legal basis is the exception clause of can. 1323 n. 4: necessity does not exempt when "the act is intrinsically evil or tends to be harmful to souls." Wagner develops this through the Exegetical Commentary, on which the clause covers acts whose contempt for faith or authority "is objective and comes from the very nature of the act or the circumstances."[19]

Three replies.

First, the act is not intrinsically evil in the received sense. An intrinsically evil act is evil from its object, independent of circumstances and of any positive precept; no necessity could ever reach it. The consecration of a bishop is in itself a sacred action; where no jurisdiction is claimed, its malice derives from the absence of a permission required by ecclesiastical positive law. I argued in my defense (§6.2[5]) that the mandate requirement is divine law only insofar as a consecration arrogates jurisdiction, which is why Pius XII grounds it in the transmission of jurisdiction,[20] and why auxiliary, titular, and emeritus bishops show that orders and jurisdiction are separable. The bishops of July 1, 2026 were consecrated, in the Society's own words, "to serve as auxiliary bishops of the Society of Saint Pius X, **without jurisdiction**."[21] An act whose malice derives from a positive-law prohibition is exactly the kind of act necessity can reach; *necessitas legem non habet* never claimed to reach further than human law. The Church's own penal history points the same way: under the 1917 Code (can. 2370) an unauthorized consecration was punished with mere suspension;[22] the excommunication dates from 1951, occasioned by state-controlled consecrations in China.[23] A penalty that fluctuates with circumstances is a strange marker of intrinsic evil.

Second, "necessary and direct contempt for authority," objective and from the nature of the act, is what the traditional definitions of schism deny of an act like this. St. Thomas: "The essence of schism consists in rebelliously disobeying the commandments: and I say 'rebelliously,' since a schismatic both obstinately scorns the commandments of the Church, and refuses to submit to her judgment."[24] Naz: schism "presupposes a systematic and habitual refusal of dependence."[25] Cajetan: "Disobedience, however stubborn, does not constitute schism unless it is a rebellion against the office of the pope or that of the Church, such that one refuses to be subject to him and to recognize him as superior."[26] The act of July 1, the consecration of auxiliaries without jurisdiction by men who profess the primacy and sign themselves the pope's "most devoted son,"[4] is grave disobedience in a single matter. On the traditional definitions, disobedience is not contempt of authority, and it is contempt that the exception clause objectively requires (my defense, §6.3[5]).

Added to this is a finding from the drafting history that bears directly on the reading of the _Exegetical Commentary_. The 1917 Code knew three limbs for the exception clause: fear and necessity did not excuse if the act was intrinsically evil "aut vergat in contemptum fidei vel ecclesiasticae auctoritatis vel in animarum damnum" (can. 2205 §3).[22] Current can. 1323 n. 4 has only two; contempt of the faith or of ecclesiastical authority has been deleted. This was deliberate. In 1981 Cardinal Siri moved to restore the deleted limb "secundum traditionem disciplinarem Ecclesiae", and the Code commission refused: "Quoad contemptum auctoritatis ecclesiasticae nemo hodie sustinet talem esse gravitatis ut metus gravis his in casibus a poena non excuset."[27] The classical commentators assigned schism precisely to this deleted limb ("in contemptum auctoritatis ecclesiasticae vergit actus ipsam auctoritatem impugnans, e. g., schisma") and understood _animarum damnum_ as harm to the souls of _others_ through complicity and scandal.[28] An interpretation that defines "harmful to souls" through an objective contempt of authority thus brings back through the back door the limb that the legislator, on express motion, declined to restore.

The penal history of the act itself shows the same. The Holy Office decree of April 9, 1951, which introduced the excommunication, covered consecrators and consecrated expressly "etsi metu gravi coacti (c. 2229, § 3, 3°)", thereby placing the act under precisely this contempt clause of the old law.[23] Can. 1382 of 1983 and can. 1387 of 2021 did not adopt this addition. And in 1981 the Code commission rejected the self-executing character of the penalty for schism with express reference to the Lefebvre case: "Exigi non potest auto-applicatio poenae (quod est characteristicum in poenis latae sententiae), si incerta est fere semper, etiam pro reo, existentia ipsa delicti."[27] Only the promulgated text of 1983 turned the draft's "excommunicetur" into a latae sententiae penalty.[29]

Third, and this deserves more attention than it gets: even if one concedes the clause, the latae sententiae penalty still fails wherever the necessity is real. Can. 1324 §1 n. 5 expressly covers one who "acted by reason of necessity... **if the offence is intrinsically evil or tends to be harmful to souls**," and §3 excludes the automatic penalty for him too.[10] The legislator anticipated Rome's own qualification and still withheld the latae sententiae penalty from the man who acts out of real necessity. The intrinsic-evil clause therefore cannot save the automatic excommunication by itself. Rome's position requires both that no necessity whatsoever existed and that the Society's belief in one was crass, supine, or affected. Neither half has, to my knowledge, ever been argued rather than asserted.

## 8. A Note on the Laity

Wagner argues, independently of the penalty, that public schismatic acts sever one from membership in the Church regardless of culpability. Whatever the merits of that thesis, it engages only after one establishes that the acts are schismatic, and that is the point in dispute, turning on the definition of schism just discussed. John Paul II's formula, "such disobedience - which implies in practice the rejection of the Roman primacy - constitutes a schismatic act,"[30] asserts the implication; Thomas, Cajetan, and Naz give reasons to deny it where jurisdiction is neither claimed nor conferred and the primacy is explicitly professed. An implication asserted is not an implication demonstrated.

For the lay faithful threatened by the 2026 Explanatory Note, the Hawaii precedent is directly on point: the CDF itself ruled in 1993 that attending SSPX chapels and even procuring an SSPX bishop for confirmations, acts "blameworthy on various accounts," were "not sufficient to constitute the crime of schism."[7] The 1996 Note, which the 2026 Note adopts for the conditions of lay adherence, was written three years after that ruling and must be read against it: whatever "formal adherence to the schism" means, Rome's own controlling application of it sets the bar far higher than attendance, sacraments, or association.

The Dicastery itself confirmed this on July 2, 2026 in a little-noticed accompanying document, the _Prassi per la riconciliazione_: "L'imposizione della pena a laici appartenenti alla Fraternità Sacerdotale San Pio X non può essere presunta in modo automatico, ma deve essere valutata caso per caso." Expressly "non sono da ritenersi imputabili" are lay people who attended the SSPX "solo per motivi liturgici o spirituali", and lay people who, though aware of the tensions with the Holy See, "non rifiutano il Magistero o l'autorità del Romano Pontefice".[31] The document is undated, bears no protocol number and no signature, and was transmitted to the nunciatures. But it shows that Rome itself does not understand the lay clause of the Explanatory Note as an automatic penalty.

## 9. Must One Submit Outwardly?

One question remains, which the title of this essay asks and which I do not want to evade. Even if the penalty is null: may one behave publicly as if it did not exist? Tradition answers in a differentiated way, and its answer is not comfortable in every respect.

For the question whether a latae sententiae penalty was incurred at all, the presumption favors the person concerned. Cappello: "In dubio an censura sit incursa, necne, sive dubium sit iuris sive facti, non tenetur quis in foro conscientiae se habere ut censuratum." At the same time he states: a manifestly null censure does not bind; the person concerned "potest se habere tamquam non censuratum, sive in foro interno sive etiam in foro externo"; but as long as the nullity is not publicly established, he is bound "per accidens" to observe it outwardly, "non quidem vi ipsius censurae, sed vi iuris naturalis, ratione scil. scandali et contemptus auctoritatis ecclesiasticae vitandi".[32] Wernz-Vidal add that this obligation lapses where the injustice is publicly and commonly known.[33] What then still binds is not the censure but the charity that avoids scandal.

This is the point at which the SSPX's position is most exposed, and I want to name it as such. Tradition does not simply give one who holds a censure null the right to disregard it publicly; it requires that the grounds of nullity be made public and that the legal path be taken. The Society has done both: it has published its reasons, and on July 11, 2026 it filed the request for revocation under cann. 1734 ff., which has suspensive effect under can. 1353, because that canon expressly covers remedies against decrees "quae poenam quamlibet irrogent vel declarent".[8] The objection that this suspensive effect concerns only the effects of the _declaration_, not those of a penalty already incurred by force of law,[34] is correct, but it does not answer the disputed question; it presupposes it: it holds only if the penalty was incurred, and that is precisely what the Society denies for the reasons set out above.

An observation about procedure belongs here. Two years earlier, the same Dicastery under the same Prefect declared the same censure for schism (can. 1364 §1) against Archbishop Carlo Maria Viganò, but only after an extrajudicial penal process under can. 1720, with citation, a deadline for defense, and a court-appointed defender; the judgment was grounded in his public refusal to recognize and submit to the pope.[35] Against six bishops, against the priests of an entire society, and against its formally adhering laity, by contrast, the 2026 declaration was issued without any process, against men who expressly recognize the pope. This does not make the declaration null of itself. But it shows that Rome knows the path of a process for establishing schism and took it where recognition of the pope was denied.

## 10. Conclusion

Wagner is right about the framework and, I believe, wrong about what follows from it. St. Thomas does teach that a valid but unjust excommunication binds, and if the 2026 excommunications are valid, that teaching applies to the Society and to me. But Thomas teaches with equal clarity that a void sentence "has no effect, for there is no excommunication," and the SSPX's entire claim is nullity: the latae sententiae penalty was excluded by the express provisions of cann. 1323 and 1324 on every branch, whether the necessity obtains (n. 4), or was believed blamelessly (n. 7), or was believed culpably (n. 8 with §3), so that the declaratory decree declares a fact that never occurred. The Hawaii Six case shows this is not a construction alien to Rome's own practice: a declaratory decree of excommunication for schism, in an SSPX matter, was examined by the Holy See and declared "null and void" because the penalty had never been incurred. Wagner's article dismisses this whole line by citing a provision it does not rest on (n. 4 alone) and a document that never examined it, and that admits it could not evaluate the canonical thesis it was rejecting.

Where is my position genuinely vulnerable? I would rather name the points than have them discovered for me.

1. **The factual question itself.** The primary claim is that the necessity objectively obtains. Rome has twice determined the contrary, and such a determination, though fallible, may not be contradicted lightly. My defense argues the presumption in favor of authority is overcome here; if the diagnosis of the crisis in my defense (§2, §4, §5) fails, the primary claim fails with it.
2. **Can. 1325.** If someone can show that the Society's belief in the state of necessity is affected ignorance in the technical sense, cultivated in order to act more freely, rather than a studied error, then the n. 8 branch fails, and with it the nullity argument in the case that no necessity obtains. I have argued the label does not fit; I hold this with real but not complete confidence.
3. **The intrinsic-evil clause.** If someone can show that an episcopal consecration without mandate is intrinsically evil in the strict sense, evil from its object and not from a positive-law prohibition, then necessity exempts nothing here, and only the error branches remain (which, note, still exclude the automatic penalty unless can. 1325 applies as well). To show this, one would have to defeat the argument of my defense (§6.2[5]) that the mandate requirement is divine law only where jurisdiction is arrogated, which the consecration of auxiliaries without jurisdiction precisely avoids.
4. **Outward observance.** Even if the penalty is null, Tradition requires that public scandal be avoided as long as the nullity is not publicly established (section 9). Whether publishing the reasons and filing the legal remedy satisfies this requirement is the question on which I am least certain.

These are the arguments that would change my mind, and I invite anyone, Wagner included, whose work I read with profit, to make them. What I do not think can stand is the shortcut: quoting St. Thomas on submission to unjust sentences against a party whose entire claim is that the sentence is null, when the quoted passage itself carves out exactly that case, and when Rome itself, within living memory and in this very matter, has declared such a sentence null.
