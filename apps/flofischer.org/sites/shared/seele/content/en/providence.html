Providence and Free Will

The Contradiction

Divine omniscience requires that, necessarily, for every proposition \(x\), if \(x\) is true, God knows \(x\):

\[ \forall x: \Box (x \Rightarrow K(G,x)) \]

1

Free will requires that, for every free decision \(y\), it would have been possible not to choose \(y\):

\[ \forall y: (y \in FW) \rightarrow \Diamond \neg y \]

2

This condition is necessary but not sufficient to characterize an act as free. A programmable robot with an if–else statement that depends on the random decay of a radioactive particle satisfies the condition above, but it still does not make a free decision.

A further necessary condition is that the person making the decision is its determining cause:

\[ \forall y: (y \in FW) \rightarrow \exists Z: y \iff C(Z,y) \]

3

To avoid saying that the radioactive particle has made a free decision, we must add that \(Z\) begins the relevant causal chain, or at least that no determining cause precedes \(Z\) within that chain. Otherwise there would merely be a determining cause with a probabilistic effect.

The tension with divine omniscience emerges once God is understood, correctly in the Aristotelian-Thomistic tradition, as absolutely simple. The Thomistic God has no passive potency, so his knowledge can only be active.4

Imagine knowing the exact position of a ball without observing it because you threw it yourself and know both the environmental influences and the physical laws perfectly. Knowing its position by observation would be passive knowledge. Knowing it through perfect knowledge of yourself, of your act of throwing, and of the world would be active knowledge.

Yet if God is to know free decisions in this way, he must be their cause; and if he knows them necessarily, he appears to be their determining cause. The formal conditions above might still call \(y\) a free decision, but it would be God’s will, not the creature’s, making the decision. God would then be morally responsible and could not justly condemn anyone. That is one of several conflicts with biblical and Catholic teaching that we must avoid.

The dilemma can be stated more explicitly: what is the truth-maker of \(y\)?5 If the human being is the truth-maker, God seems to lack perfect knowledge. If God is the truth-maker, the human being is not free. If both are involved, the dilemma remains, because in divergent decisions one determining contribution must ultimately prevail over the other.

Molinism

Molinism attempts to solve the problem through middle knowledge: God knows how every person would respond in every possible situation. Since he knows the actual circumstances, he therefore knows how that person acts in the actual world.

The very possibility of middle knowledge seems to conflict with the first condition of free will. It appears to presuppose that in every possible world either \(y\) or \(\neg y\) is necessary. Put differently: what makes the counterfactual propositions contained in God’s middle knowledge true? We arrive at the same dilemma, with human freedom sacrificed to divine omniscience.

Báñez’s Position

I cannot give this view much attention here. Merely asserting that God can, by means of physical premotions, be the determining cause of a decision without thereby making it any less the person’s free decision does not make the apparent oxymoron disappear.

Open Theism

The proposed solution is open theism. God is analogous to the programmer of a game in which the player can make genuine decisions. The programmer knows the whole source code, every point at which the player can choose, and what the world will look like after each choice. He can plan how to intervene in response to the player’s decisions. He knows every possible branch of the decision tree.

Leaving the analogy and speaking directly about God, we can distinguish three kinds of divine knowledge: necessary internal knowledge, actual internal knowledge, and external knowledge.

Necessary internal knowledge encompasses necessary truths: tautologies, mathematical truths, knowledge of God’s necessary nature, and—following from the latter—knowledge of every possible world. Since God perfectly knows himself, he knows every possible way in which creation could reflect him.

God has actual internal knowledge because, in his absolutely simple being, he is identical with his act of creation, which is a free act. He knows every physically necessary truth, the constitution of the universe at the moment of creation, and all of his own direct interventions under the relevant conditions.

Free decisions belong to external knowledge.

External Knowledge

Every true proposition that is not part of God’s internal knowledge belongs to his external knowledge. Such propositions describe the relation of the created world to God. If \(x\) is true but not internal to God, we may say that God knows \(x\) insofar as he is the first and sustaining cause of the truth-maker of \(x\), and can act in dependence on \(x\).

Whatever one thinks of open theism, a Thomist must hold that at least some divine knowledge is external in this sense. I give several arguments against eternalism below. If presentism or the growing-block theory of time is true, the present has an objective status. Facts then have objective truth-values that depend on the present.

“Olaf Scholz is Chancellor of the Federal Republic of Germany,” for example, is a proposition whose objective truth-value changes with time. Insofar as it is objectively true, God must know it; insofar as it changes over time, it cannot be internal to God. The Thomist is therefore committed to placing at least this kind of proposition within God’s external knowledge. Open theism asks only that free decisions—and sometimes their consequences—belong to this class as well.

Objection 1: External Knowledge Is Not Really Knowledge

The objection is that it would be incorrect to say God actually knows \(x\) merely because he:

  1. is the first and sustaining cause of \(x\); and
  2. can act in dependence on \(x\).

This objection is not simply mistaken. We are not saying that there is literally some distinct thing called knowledge in God—but neither do we say that there is a distinct thing called strength in him. For the Thomist, God is his act of existence and contains no differentiable attributes. To say that God knows something will always differ from saying that a creature knows something.

The objection—probably the most obvious and forceful one—arises from applying our mode of knowledge to God univocally rather than analogically.6

This definition of external knowledge may even satisfy St. Thomas’s account of divine knowledge of future contingents.7 On at least one reading of Thomas, our definition preserves his conditions: God knows future contingents both in their causes, insofar as he is their first cause, and in their existence, insofar as he is their sustaining cause and their essences partially reflect his own.

Objection 2: Prophecy

It seems that God could not prophesy on the basis of external knowledge. In one respect that is correct: God does not know all propositions in the set of his external knowledge in a way that permits him to reveal every one of them at an earlier moment without falsehood.

Yet, like the programmer in the analogy, God has many ways of directing events. He can restrict the alternatives among which a free person chooses; he can influence the will in various ways; and he can override it, in which case the person is no longer morally responsible. God can therefore guide history toward certain fixed events and reveal them in advance.

Even those who reject open theism must admit limits of this kind. Under Molinism it may be true that \(Z\) chooses \(x\) only if \(x\) is not revealed to \(Z\) beforehand; God then cannot reveal \(x\) to \(Z\). A follower of Báñez appears at least to face infinity-Bernadette paradoxes.8 My form of open theism, combined with my preferred theory of time, should avoid these paradoxes.

Biblical Support for Open Theism

The philosophical claim that open theism is the only theory capable of consistently combining an omniscient, absolutely simple God with genuinely free human beings was already sufficient to persuade me. Yet I also think open theism is, prima facie, the natural biblical account of providence.

Scripture is full of episodes in which God tests human beings. Only under open theism are these tests in the most straightforward sense genuine tests. Under Molinism, God already knows exactly how each person would respond in every circumstance; under Báñez’s position, the human being is not ultimately the one determining the decision. Other purposes can of course be assigned to biblical tests within those systems, but such explanations are not their natural prima facie reading. I regard this as an exceptionally strong argument for open theism, even if the brevity of this paragraph understates its force.

Concluding Thoughts

Father Deneke, I unfortunately have still not had time to read your book in full. What I have read, however, surprised me very positively. You appear to be one of the few philosophers who approaches this subject with genuine intellectual honesty: you seem to think the theory through for yourself rather than forcing a defense of a favored philosopher’s system—as Ed Feser sometimes seems to do.

I was also encouraged that you allow, properly understood, that God can limit himself. I suspect my theory requires this as well.

To understand my approach better, it is worth reading Rob Koons’s “Dual Agency: A Thomistic Account of Providence and Human Freedom.”9 I agree with nearly everything through page 13, especially Koons’s criticism of Molinism, and with the objections he raises against his own position on page 14. I also agree with his praise for van Inwagen’s critique of compatibilism.

Koons’s theory loses me because it does not identify a truth-maker that is simultaneously:

  1. possible;
  2. plausible; and
  3. common to propositions (2) and (3).

It therefore certainly does not identify one that plausibly preserves human freedom.

I will now show how I attempt to explain two basic theological doctrines within my account, and conclude with a brief presentation of my arguments concerning theories of time.

The Betrayal of Judas

How could Jesus foresee Judas’s betrayal at the Last Supper?

I believe that, through many prior sinful decisions, Judas had so darkened his soul and narrowed his own freedom that, in the decisive situation, he could no longer do otherwise than betray God. Jesus knew the betrayal in advance because Judas’s freedom had become so restricted; Judas nevertheless remained responsible because he had brought about that restriction through his own earlier decisions. Sin resembles addiction: by a sequence of free choices, one enters a downward spiral in which one progressively constrains one’s freedom.

Mary’s Sinlessness

It appears that at the Annunciation God knew Mary would remain free from sin. How can Mary deserve praise—and thus be responsible—if God knew this beforehand?

One might argue that my theory fails here, but I do not think the competing theories possess an explanatory advantage. I suspect that God created Mary’s soul before her conception and, as with the angels, placed before her a choice to follow him. Through her “yes,” he preserved her from sin at her conception and in every later decision. God therefore knew her sinlessness, while she remained praiseworthy because of her pre-conception “yes.”

I am aware that this sounds highly unorthodox—in the colloquial sense, not necessarily in the sense of heterodox. Yet the theory has helped me love Mary more, because it lets us understand her contribution to the Incarnation as merit in the fullest possible sense. Under other theories she can seem less genuinely praiseworthy when she says, “Let it be done to me according to your word.” This is worth reflecting upon in prayer.

Theories of Time

Given the nature of act and potency, presentism is, in Ed Feser’s view as well, the natural Thomistic position.10

Eternalism appears to lead to fatalism, as Aristotle suggests in his sea-battle argument.11 Under presentism, a proposition about a future contingent event cannot yet have a truth-value, because it cannot correspond to anything actually existent: the future is pure potency. Fatalism is thereby avoided.

On the same reasoning, however, past facts lose their truth-values as well. The growing-block theory of time preserves, within a correspondence theory of truth, the truth of past facts without committing us to present truth-values for future contingents.
