1. Modernism

Pope Pius X writes in his encyclical Pascendi dominici gregis (PDG):[1]

Original: “Hätte sich jemand zur Aufgabe gestellt, die Quintessenz aller Glaubensirrtümer, die es je gegeben hat, zusammenzutragen, so hätte er es nicht besser machen können, als es die Modernisten getan haben.”

Translation: “Had someone set himself the task of gathering together the quintessence of all the errors in faith that have ever existed, he could not have done it better than the Modernists have done.”

Why, then, is Modernism precisely this quintessence of all heresies? Pope Pius X never defines Modernism directly; he only gives case studies of it. The philosopher who, in my opinion, has grasped the core of Modernism best is, ironically, not a Catholic: I am speaking of Julius Evola.[2] In Evola’s works, especially in Revolt Against the Modern World, Modernism appears as a reduction of the world to the realm of “becoming,” while the realm of “being” is denied altogether. In a Thomistic interpretation of Evola’s works, it becomes clear why this definition hits the core of Modernism, also with respect to Catholic doctrine. For Saint Thomas, God is precisely Being itself, the subsisting act of existing. Everything created is being only insofar as it participates in the pure Being of God. The truth, goodness, and beauty of everything created exist in the created thing only insofar as it participates in God and is ordered to Him, in whom truth, goodness, and beauty are one in His Being as a categorical limiting case.[3] Whoever denies this also lacks an ultimate telos toward which creation is ordered. This is exactly what the Modernist does: he reduces the world to the realm of becoming, even to imperfect, often purely materialistic things. Since he lacks the realm of being, he must seek the telos of all created things within creation itself. He reduces ethics to a maximization of social happiness. He reduces artifacts to pragmatic utility, in which aesthetics has no value in itself. He must reduce objective truths to subjective feelings. Finally, he also views religion only in relation to creation rather than with respect to the Creator. It is precisely this discrepancy that makes Modernism the quintessence of all heresies.

2. The Church Crisis of Modernism

In PDG, Pope Pius X gives many examples of how Modernism manifests itself with respect to the Church. He states that, from the perspective of philosophy, it expresses itself in agnosticism. Here Pius X does not mean a weak agnosticism that abstains from making statements about the existence of God, but a strong agnosticism, one that denies on principle that man can know the existence of God. This denial of natural theology leads them to seek the foundation of religion in man, more specifically in his subconscious, in his feelings. By relocating the foundation of religion into man’s feelings, religion becomes subjective and leads to relativism with respect to the truth claims of individual religions. Likewise, dogma and doctrine are, at their core, referred back to these feelings and thus themselves become subjective and changeable. Pius X published this encyclical in 1907, which shows how long Modernism has already permeated the Church. That this situation has certainly not improved to the present day, but has become much worse, should be obvious to every attentive Catholic. Likewise, it should be obvious to every Catholic how Modernism endangers the salvation of souls. Extra Ecclesiam nulla salus (EENS) is a dogma of the Church. Outside the Catholic Church there is no salvation. People who are invincibly ignorant of the truth of the Catholic Church can, of course, still be saved, just as people who are invincibly ignorant of Jesus Christ can be saved. But that does not change the principle. People consciously hide from God, Jesus, His Church, and her teachings so as not to have to confront their own sin. Whoever outwardly advocates subjectivism with respect to the Church and her teachings becomes complicit in this and endangers the salvation of these people’s souls. A good example of this is William Lane Craig. Craig is an evangelical philosopher, theologian, and apologist. In 2021 he was ranked nineteenth among the most influential philosophers of the last thirty years and fourth among theologians.[4] Craig has devoted his entire adult life to the study of philosophy and theology. Yet he apparently holds the view that Catholic teaching with respect to EENS changed at the Second Vatican Council (V2), and that there is no danger for him in being outside the Catholic Church. In the Q&A portion of his Sunday school, he answered:

“As I understand the documents of the Second Vatican Council that was held in the 60s, the Catholic Church now views Protestants quite differently than it used to. […] for someone like myself who is sort of this benighted Protestant and doesn’t recognize that, I am not held guilty for not being a part of the Roman Catholic Church because I really am in a sense”

I will return later in more detail to the relevant passages from V2, but I think this example already shows how an outwardly advocated relativism can pose a danger to the salvation of people’s souls. 3. The Resistance of the FSSPX

The resistance of the FSSPX to Modernism in the Church is shown above all in three points:

  1. Refusal to sign all the documents of V2
  2. Refusal to celebrate the Novus Ordo (NO)
  3. The ordination of priests and consecration of bishops without the consent of ecclesiastical authorities

In both doctrine and discipline there are levels of obedience owed to ecclesiastical authority. To deny an infallible dogma is sin and leads to a direct rupture with the Catholic Church. Prudential judgments or pastoral assessments, by contrast, may be contradicted, provided they have been subjected to serious and respectful engagement. Likewise, one must always submit to divine law, but one may, for example, refuse obedience to acts of governance under certain circumstances. The degree to which one owes obedience derives directly from our epistemic access to the divine truth behind the teachings and disciplines. It is doctrine that the Catholic Church cannot err in questions of faith and morals when she binds all the faithful to them, as when, for example, a pope proclaims a dogma ex cathedra. Here, therefore, we have direct infallible access to a divine truth; because we can be certain that it is true, we must of course submit. When a pope, bishop, or priest only judges prudentially, he can err. By reason of his office and study, we have good indications for believing that he teaches truth, but none of that guarantees it, which is why we may under certain circumstances contradict him. I will later discuss where the boundary lies with the ordinary magisterium. The same also applies to disciplinary obedience. If an authority proclaims divine law, then we have epistemic certainty that the act is ordered to the good. If his directive is a pure act of governance, we have no divine guarantee that the directive is in fact ordered to the good. If it is in fact evil, we may refuse the act. For all three points in which the FSSPX refuses obedience, the question now arises whether the resistance is justified. In all three, the issue is to avert the danger to the salvation of souls posed by Modernism.

4. The Documents of the Second Vatican Council

Before we turn to the specific passages of V2 to which the FSSPX refuses assent, we should discuss whether the nature of the passages allows contradiction at all in principle.

4.1. When Obedience Can Be Refused

The levels of certainty and the corresponding levels of submission that a Catholic owes them are in fact somewhat more complicated than previously indicated. Historically, there was also a development here. Ludwig Ott, in his Fundamentals of Catholic Dogma, for example, arranged them as theological notes, from positive notes beginning with fides divina and descending in authority to negative censures ending with propositio haeretica. There has never been a single generally accepted taxonomy. After V2, however, over the years the following classification crystallized (cf. [9], p. 190 ff., and [10]):

Kind of Statement Nature Response Called For
1. Dogma Infallibly defined by the Church as divinely revealed Theological faith (i.e., divine and catholic faith)
2. Other Infallible Statement Infallibly defined by the Church but not defined as divinely revealed Definitive assent
3. Other Doctrine Authoritatively taught by the Church Religious submission of will and intellect
4. Theological Opinion Theological view proposed for our consideration Respectful and appreciative consideration
5. Other Non-Doctrinal Statement Non-theological view Respectful and appreciative consideration

It is uncontroversial to say that the relevant passages in V2 fall into category 3. This category demands religious obedience of intellect and will. Unlike dogma and doctrine, the required response here is not derived from the truth of the statement, but from obedience to the ecclesiastical authority that proclaims it. Must this response now always be given, or can there be exceptions? Pope Benedict XVI, in his function as head of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith before his pontificate, seems explicitly to contradict this view. In Donum Veritatis, he lists reasons that would justify dissent and rejects them all. He also presents the following as a false understanding of non-infallible doctrine:[11]

Original: “Häufiger wird die Meinung vertreten, der Theologe sei nur dem unfehlbaren Lehramt zu folgen gehalten, während nach Art eines gewissen theologischen Positivismus die ohne Inanspruchnahme des Charismas der Unfehlbarkeit vorgelegten Lehren keinerlei verpflichtenden Charakter hätten […]”

Translation: “More frequently, the opinion is advanced that the theologian is bound to follow only the infallible magisterium, while, in the manner of a certain theological positivism, teachings presented without recourse to the charism of infallibility would have no binding character whatsoever […]”

Does Ratzinger therefore exclude every form of dissent from non-infallible doctrine? I do not think so, and I think the text, when read carefully, draws a different picture. The definition of non-infallible doctrine alone allows the possibility of error. How, then, could such doctrine per se oblige obedience? The only possibility would be if the truth value of the statement were not naturally knowable per se. As soon as it is possible to know the truth value of a statement, the obligation toward truth stands higher than obedience toward the Church. With infallible doctrine there is no tension here; with doctrine that is in principle fallible, however, there is. Some passages in Donum Veritatis read as though Ratzinger is aware of this but does not wish to say it openly, in order not to pour water on the mill of heterodox “Catholics” and not to diminish the importance of religious obedience of intellect and will. The first indication of this is the full quotation from 33:

Original: “Häufiger wird die Meinung vertreten, der Theologe sei nur dem unfehlbaren Lehramt zu folgen gehalten, während nach Art eines gewissen theologischen Positivismus die ohne Inanspruchnahme des Charismas der Unfehlbarkeit vorgelegten Lehren keinerlei verpflichtenden Charakter hätten, wobei dem einzelnen volle Freiheit gelassen würde, ihnen anzuhängen oder nicht. So sei der Theologe völlig frei, nicht unfehlbare Lehren des Magisteriums, zumal bei Einzelnormen der Moral in Zweifel zu ziehen oder abzulehnen, und durch eine derartige kritische Opposition könne er sogar zum Fortschritt der Lehre beitragen.”

Translation: “More frequently, the opinion is advanced that the theologian is bound to follow only the infallible magisterium, while, in the manner of a certain theological positivism, teachings proposed without invoking the charism of infallibility would have no binding character whatsoever, with the individual being left full freedom to adhere to them or not. Thus the theologian would be completely free to call into question or reject non-infallible teachings of the Magisterium, especially in particular norms of morality, and through such critical opposition he could even contribute to the progress of doctrine.”

Here Ratzinger is paraphrasing a position that he then rejects in the document. What is important here are the words I have put in bold. Ratzinger does not condemn the claim that the teachings have “no” binding character, but that they have “no binding character whatsoever.” He does not condemn the claim that the theologian would be left “freedom,” but that he would be left “full freedom” not to adhere to non-infallible teachings. He does not condemn the claim that the theologian is “free” to reject non-infallible teachings, but that he is “completely free.” Another indicator is in 24, where Ratzinger writes:

Original: “Der Wille, einem Spruch des Lehramts bei an sich nicht irreformablen Dingen loyal zuzustimmen, muß die Regel sein.”

Translation: “The will to give loyal assent to a pronouncement of the Magisterium in matters which are not in themselves irreformable must be the rule.”

With this, he obviously leaves open the possibility that there can also be an exception to the rule.[12] Later in the paragraph Ratzinger writes:

Original: “Aber man würde in Gegensatz zur Wahrheit geraten, wollte man aus einigen bestimmten Fällen schließen, das Lehramt der Kirche könne sich bei seinen Klugheitsurteilen gewöhnlich täuschen, oder es würde sich nicht des göttlichen Beistands erfreuen […]”

Translation: “But one would come into opposition to the truth if one wished to conclude from certain particular cases that the Church’s Magisterium could be habitually mistaken in its prudential judgments, or that it did not enjoy divine assistance […]”

Here too, in my opinion, Ratzinger’s intention becomes obvious in his formulations: he is aware of the danger that, as soon as one admits the possibility of errors, one opens the door to subjectivism. Therefore he must also emphasize here that admitting the possibility of errors does not mean that this could occur habitually. For this reason, the Catholic apologist Jimmy Akin also concludes his chapter on non-infallible doctrine in Teaching with Authority with the paragraph:[13]

“We thus see that the possibility of error in non-infallible teachings is a real one, and one about which the Magisterium is becoming increasingly frank. However, the Holy Spirit still guides the Magisterium when formulating such teachings. Although he does not guarantee complete freedom from error in the Church’s non-definitive teaching, he ensures the content of the deposit of faith is preserved and its essential principles remain present in the Church’s teaching - even if there is an admixture of ideas that needs to be purified through doctrinal development, which also takes place under his guidance. Consequently, one cannot infer “that the Church’s Magisterium can be habitually mistaken in its prudential judgments,” and its non-infallible teachings ordinarily require the “religious assent of will and intellect””

He explicitly leaves open the possibility of non-ordinary cases in which one can refuse religious obedience of intellect and will. But this possibility should not trivialize what such a refusal means in ordinary cases, nor should it play down the necessary weight of circumstances that are prerequisites for such disobedience.

4.1.1. A Historical Case Study

The best historical precedent for a refusal of submission to the ordinary magisterium is found in the Arian crisis. It also underscores how grave a situation must be for refusal to be justified. At the same time, however, the case also shows that the Church can err in serious matters when she does not teach infallibly. In the Arian dispute, the issue was the words Homoiousios and Homoousios with respect to the essence of Christ. The first formulation meant that Jesus was similar in essence to God the Father; the second, that He was of the same essence. The former was the heretical Arian formula; the latter was the orthodox formula defended by Athanasius against the Arians. Here is a chronology of the events:

  • 325: The Council of Nicaea condemns Arianism and formulates the homoousios (consubstantiality of Father and Son).
  • Around 335/336: Athanasius is deposed at the Council of Tyre and exiled by Emperor Constantine.
  • 352: Liberius becomes pope and at first defends Athanasius and the Nicene doctrine.
  • 353/354: Council of Arles: bishops, including papal legates, condemn Athanasius; only a few resist.
  • 355: Council of Milan: almost all bishops condemn Athanasius; Liberius is summoned by the emperor to Milan, refuses the condemnation, and is exiled to Beroea in Thrace.
  • End of 357/beginning of 358: Liberius gives in during exile. He condemns Athanasius and signs the First Formula of Sirmium (351), which avoids the homoousios but can be interpreted orthodoxly.
  • Liberius did not agree to the radically subordinationist Second Formula of Sirmium (357); he condemned extreme Anomoeans and later supported semi-Arian formulations such as “like in all things” (homoiousios-like).
  • Hilary of Poitiers actively resisted the Arian formulas and the exiling emperor; he wrote critical works. He quoted letters of Liberius and sharply criticized his weakness as perfidia, betrayal.
  • Around 358/359 Hilary insulted/criticized Liberius indirectly through his writings because Liberius had abandoned Athanasius and had partly agreed to Arian formulas; from Hilary’s perspective this was a betrayal of the Nicene cause.
  • 359: Council of Rimini: 400 bishops sign a semi-Arian formula. At the same time, the same occurs in an eastern council.
  • After 359/360: Liberius returns to the Nicene line, grants forgiveness under conditions to those who fell away at the Council of Rimini, and demands the profession of Nicaea.

(cf. [14] and [15]) Here, then, we have an example in which two saints publicly resisted what we would today call the ordinary magisterium, and were later canonized, among other things, for it. The fact that the pope never explicitly agreed to the formulas of the Councils of Rimini and Seleucia does not prevent us from describing this today as ordinary magisterium. Moreover, one could argue that Liberius at least implicitly agreed with the Arians through his condemnation of Athanasius, even if under pressure. At least from the perspective of the anti-Arians, Athanasius then stood against the majority of the bishops and the pope, which is also obvious from the fact that Hilary insulted Liberius in a letter for his betrayal of Athanasius.

4.1.2. Alternative Prerequisite for Dissent

As mentioned above, the taxonomy of levels of authority changed in V2 itself and became stricter. Thus one can question the development in V2 toward stricter claims on assent to doctrine itself. In the introduction to Ott’s Fundamentals of Catholic Dogma, it says:[16]

“The ordinary and usual form of the Papal teaching activity is not infallible. Further, the decisions of the Roman Congregations (Holy Office, Bible Commission) are not infallible. Nevertheless normally they are to be accepted with an inner assent which is based on the high supernatural authority of the Holy See (assensus intemus supematuralis, assensus religiosus). The so-called “ silentium obsequiosum,” that is “ reverent silence,” does not generally suffice. By way of exception, the obligation of inner agreement may cease if a competent expert, after a renewed scientific investigation of all grounds, arrives at the positive conviction that the decision rests on an error.”

He therefore sets the bar much lower. For a competent expert, dissent from non-infallible ordinary magisterium simply requires that, after thorough study, he arrive at a different conclusion from the Magisterium. This position is not an idiosyncrasy of Ott’s, but the consensus of pre-conciliar manual theology. Msgr. Gerard van Noort, one of the leading dogmatic theologians of the early twentieth century and, with his ten-volume Tractatus apologetici et dogmatici, for decades a standard seminary textbook, writes in The Sources of Revelation (Dogmatic Theology Vol. III, ed. Castelot/Murphy, Newman Press 1961, n. 254, p. 237):

“Granted the need for submission to the authentic Magisterium, it still remains true that just as a merely authentic proposal is by its very nature incomplete and provisory, so, too, is the religious assent due to it.”

Religious assent to non-infallible magisterium is therefore by its nature incomplete and provisional, not absolute. Van Noort himself sets the threshold for suspending this assent somewhat higher than Ott. He calls it “extremely rare” and limits it to theologically trained persons who present their objections to the Holy See and meanwhile observe “reverential silence” (ibid., n. 254). But in the basic principle Ott and Van Noort agree that suspension is possible and legitimate under certain conditions. Thus we have two independent witnesses from the manualist tradition for the same finding: non-infallible magisterium does not demand the definitive assent that belongs only to the infallible, and under qualified conditions it can even lead to dissent.

4.2. The Critical Formulas in Vatican II

The FSSPX has no official list of passages from V2 that it rejects. Here, however, I want to list the passages most frequently mentioned.

4.2.1. Dignitatis humanae (DH)

In Dignitatis humanae (DH), the FSSPX objects especially to 2, where it says:[17]

Original: “Das Vatikanische Konzil erklärt, daß die menschliche Person das Recht auf religiöse Freiheit hat. Diese Freiheit besteht darin, daß alle Menschen frei sein müssen von jedem Zwang sowohl von seiten Einzelner wie gesellschaftlicher Gruppen, wie jeglicher menschlichen Gewalt, so daß in religiösen Dingen niemand gezwungen wird, gegen sein Gewissen zu handeln, noch daran gehindert wird, privat und öffentlich, als einzelner oder in Verbindung mit anderen - innerhalb der gebührenden Grenzen - nach seinem Gewissen zu handeln. Ferner erklärt das Konzil, das Recht auf religiöse Freiheit sei in Wahrheit auf die Würde der menschlichen Person selbst gegründet, so wie sie durch das geoffenbarte Wort Gottes und durch die Vernunft selbst erkannt wird.”

Translation: “The Vatican Council declares that the human person has a right to religious freedom. This freedom consists in this, that all men must be free from every coercion, both on the part of individuals and social groups and of any human power whatever, so that in religious matters no one is forced to act against his conscience, nor is anyone prevented from acting according to his conscience privately and publicly, alone or in association with others - within due limits. Furthermore, the Council declares that the right to religious freedom is truly founded on the dignity of the human person itself, as known through the revealed Word of God and by reason itself.”

The Society of St. Pius X naturally agrees that no one may be forced against his conscience to convert. It objects, however, to the statement that no one may be prevented from practicing his religion and that this is a natural right of man. Non-Catholic religions preach untruth and falsehood and therefore, qua false religion, can have no rights. Still less can such rights have their origin in human nature created by God.[18] DH does qualify this with the words within due limits, but the definition of these due limits shows precisely the problem. For in 7 it says:

Original: “Da die bürgerliche Gesellschaft außerdem das Recht hat, sich gegen Mißbräuche zu schützen, die unter dem Vorwand der Religionsfreiheit vorkommen können, so steht es besonders der Staatsgewalt zu, diesen Schutz zu gewähren; dies darf indessen nicht auf willkürliche Weise oder durch unbillige Begünstigung einer Partei geschehen, sondern nur nach rechtlichen Normen, die der objektiven sittlichen Ordnung entsprechen und wie sie für den wirksamen Rechtsschutz im Interesse aller Bürger und ihrer friedvollen Eintracht erforderlich sind, auch für die hinreichende Sorge um jenen ehrenhaften öffentlichen Frieden, der in einem geordneten Zusammenleben in wahrer Gerechtigkeit besteht, und schließlich für die pflichtgemäße Wahrung der öffentlichen Sittlichkeit. Dies alles gehört zum grundlegenden Wesensbestand des Gemeinwohls und fällt unter den Begriff der öffentlichen Ordnung. Im übrigen soll in der Gesellschaft eine ungeschmälerte Freiheit walten, wonach dem Menschen ein möglichst weiter Freiheitsraum zuerkannt werden muß, und sie darf nur eingeschränkt werden, wenn und soweit es notwendig ist.”

Translation: “Since civil society, moreover, has the right to protect itself against abuses which can occur under the pretext of religious freedom, it belongs especially to public authority to provide this protection; this, however, must not be done arbitrarily or by unfair favoring of one party, but only according to legal norms which correspond to the objective moral order and are required for the effective protection of rights in the interest of all citizens and their peaceful concord, also for sufficient care for that honorable public peace which consists in ordered living together in true justice, and finally for the dutiful preservation of public morality. All this belongs to the fundamental essential content of the common good and falls under the concept of public order. For the rest, an undiminished freedom should prevail in society, according to which man must be granted the widest possible sphere of freedom, and it may be restricted only when and insofar as necessary.”

This means, then, that the limits are to be defined only with respect to society, not with respect to God. This leads DH de facto to ascribe to the individual a right, for example, to the public utterance of blasphemy, provided it does not impair public order and morality, and it denies the state any right to suppress this blasphemy. This state indifference toward religions has been condemned multiple times in the Catholic tradition. Thus Pope Pius IX condemned in the Syllabus errorum (SE), among other propositions:[19]

Original: “77. In unserer Zeit ist es nicht mehr nützlich, daß die katholische Religion unter Ausschluß aller andern Culte als einzige Staatsreligion gelte.”

Translation: “77. In our time it is no longer useful that the Catholic religion, to the exclusion of all other cults, should be regarded as the sole religion of the state.”

Original: “78. Es ist daher zu loben, daß in gewissen katholischen Ländern gesetzlich verordnet ist, daß den Einwanderern die öffentliche Ausübung ihres Cultes, welcher er auch sei, gestattet sein solle.”

Translation: “78. It is therefore praiseworthy that in certain Catholic countries it is legally decreed that immigrants should be permitted the public exercise of their cult, whatever it may be.”

The contradiction becomes even more explicit in Quanta cura (QC), the encyclical connected with SE. There it says:[20]

Original: “Von dieser absolut falschen Vorstellung über die Regierung des Staates, scheuen sie sich nicht, die irrige Meinung zu begünstigen, welche für die katholische Kirche und das Heil der Seelen im höchsten Grad zum Untergang führt, die bereits Unser unmittelbarer Vorgänger seligen Andenkens, Gregor XVI., als Wahnsinn bezeichnet hat, und zwar, die Gewissens- und Religionsfreiheit sei das eigene Recht eines jeden Menschen. Dieses Recht müsse das Gesetz in jeder wohlgeordneten Gesellschaft proklamieren und sicherstellen. Für die Bürger bestehe ein Recht auf eine allgemeine Freiheit, die weder durch die kirchliche, noch durch die staatliche Autorität eingeschränkt werden darf, und die ihnen erlaubt, ihre Ansichten und Empfindungen durch das gesprochene Wort, durch Druckschriften, oder auf andere Weise offen bekanntzugeben und zu erklären.”

Translation: “From this utterly false notion about the government of the state, they do not hesitate to foster the erroneous opinion, most ruinous for the Catholic Church and the salvation of souls, which Our immediate predecessor of blessed memory, Gregory XVI, already called madness: namely, that freedom of conscience and religion is the proper right of every man. This right must be proclaimed and secured by law in every well-ordered society. Citizens have a right to a general freedom which may be restricted neither by ecclesiastical nor by civil authority, and which allows them openly to make known and declare their views and feelings by spoken word, by printed writings, or in any other way.”

This passage reads like an exact opposite of DH: not only with respect to the rights and duties of the state, but also specifically with respect to the claim that these are human rights and not mere civil rights. The liberal progressive canon lawyer Dr. Reinhold Sebott SJ, in “Dignitatis humanae” und “Quanta cura”: Die Verurteilung der Religionsfreiheit vor dem Zweiten Vatikanischen Konzil, lists a whole series of historical contradictions between DH and traditional Catholic teaching. In a footnote there he even agrees with the FSSPX position on the contradiction between DH and Tradition, even though he draws the wrong consequences from it.[21]

4.2.2. Lumen gentium (LG) VIII

The most glaring example in my opinion - this classification as the “most glaring example” is not necessarily shared by the FSSPX - is found in LG 8:[22]

Original: “die einzige Kirche Christi, die wir im Glaubensbekenntnis als die eine, heilige, katholische und apostolische bekennen […] ist verwirklicht in der katholischen Kirche, die vom Nachfolger Petri und von den Bischöfen in Gemeinschaft mit ihm geleitet wird”

Translation: “the sole Church of Christ, which in the Creed we profess to be one, holy, catholic, and apostolic […] is realized in the Catholic Church, governed by the successor of Peter and by the bishops in communion with him”

The “verwirklicht” in the text, the Latin subsistit, that is, subsists, is a departure from the traditional est, that is, is, which teaches a strict equivalence between the Church of Christ and the Catholic Church. This est is found most prominently in Mystici Corporis Christi (Part 1):[23]

Original: “Bei einer Wesenserklärung dieser wahren Kirche Christi, welche die heilige, katholische, apostolische, römische Kirche ist kann nichts Vornehmeres und Vorzüglicheres, nichts Göttlicheres gefunden werden als jener Ausdruck, womit sie als”der mystische Leib Jesu Christi” bezeichnet wird.”

Translation: “In giving a definition of this true Church of Christ, which is the holy, catholic, apostolic, Roman Church, nothing more noble and more excellent, nothing more divine can be found than that expression by which she is designated as ‘the Mystical Body of Jesus Christ.’”

This statement is supported once again by Pope Pius XII, who criticizes in Humani generis (HG) 27:

Original: “Einige halten sich nicht gebunden an die vor einigen Jahren in einem Rundschreiben erklärte Lehre, die sich auf die Quellen der”Offenbarung” stützt und erklärt, dass der geheimnisvolle Leib Christi und die Römische katholische Kirche ein und dasselbe seien”

Translation: “Some do not consider themselves bound by the doctrine explained a few years ago in an encyclical, which rests on the sources of Revelation and declares that the Mystical Body of Christ and the Roman Catholic Church are one and the same.”

The change in the understanding of the Church is summarized by the Catholic apologist Jimmy Akin as follows:[24]

Original: “Die Verwendung von „subsistiert in“ anstelle von „ist“ hat nach dem Konzil eine breite Diskussion ausgelöst. Im Jahr 2007 veröffentlichte die Glaubenskongregation ein Dokument mit mehreren Dubia zu diesem Thema. Auf die erste Frage „Hat das Zweite Vatikanische Konzil die katholische Lehre über die Kirche verändert [mutare]?“ antwortete die Kongregation: Das Zweite Vatikanische Konzil hat diese Lehre weder verändert noch beabsichtigt, sie zu verändern, sondern sie weiterentwickelt, vertieft und umfassender erklärt (Antworten auf einige Fragen zu bestimmten Aspekten der Lehre über die Kirche, 1).

Hier weist die Kongregation darauf hin, dass das Konzil keine grundlegende Veränderung der Lehre vorgenommen hat. Dennoch - wenn man es offen sagt - hat durch die Weiterentwicklung, Vertiefung und umfassendere Erklärung eine Veränderung bestimmter Art stattgefunden. Auf die Frage „Was bedeutet die Aussage, dass die Kirche Christi in der katholischen Kirche subsistiert?“ antwortete die Kongregation: „Subsistenz“ bedeutet diese fortdauernde, geschichtliche Kontinuität und das Bleiben aller von Christus eingesetzten Elemente in der katholischen Kirche, in der die Kirche Christi auf dieser Erde konkret anzutreffen ist. Nach katholischer Lehre kann man zu Recht sagen, dass die Kirche Christi in den Kirchen und kirchlichen Gemeinschaften, die noch nicht in voller Gemeinschaft mit der katholischen Kirche stehen, aufgrund der in ihnen vorhandenen Elemente der Heiligung und der Wahrheit gegenwärtig und wirksam ist. Dennoch kann das Wort „subsistiert“ nur der katholischen Kirche allein zugeschrieben werden, weil es sich auf das Merkmal der Einheit bezieht, das wir im Glaubensbekenntnis bekennen (Ich glaube … an die eine Kirche); und diese „eine“ Kirche subsistiert in der katholischen Kirche (ebd., 2).

Schließlich heißt es auf die Frage „Warum wurde der Ausdruck ‚subsistiert in‘ anstelle des einfachen Wortes ‚ist‘ gewählt?“: Er stammt daher und bringt deutlicher zum Ausdruck, dass es „zahlreiche Elemente der Heiligung und der Wahrheit“ gibt, die sich außerhalb ihres Gefüges finden, die aber „als der Kirche Christi eigene Gaben auf die katholische Einheit hindrängen“ (ebd., 3).

Durch die Verwendung von „subsistiert in“ wollte das Konzil also ein komplexeres Verhältnis zwischen der Kirche Christi und der katholischen Kirche zum Ausdruck bringen, als es das einfache „ist“ vermittelt hätte. Damit hat eine lehrmäßige Weiterentwicklung stattgefunden. Im Unterschied zur früheren Entwicklung - bei der das Konzil statt von „Zugehörigkeit“ von „voller Eingliederung“ sprach und damit das Wort „Mitglied“ in seinem hergebrachten Sinn weiter verwendbar blieb - nimmt das Konzil hier eine Veränderung vor, die den Kernbestand einer bestehenden Lehre berührt. Es ist (außer in der Umgangssprache, in der „subsistiert“ nicht verstanden würde) nicht mehr möglich, eine so einfache Identifikation der Kirche Christi mit der katholischen Kirche vorzunehmen. Der Kern der früheren Lehre bleibt erhalten: Christus hat auf Erden nur eine Kirche gestiftet, die katholische Kirche ist die organische Fortsetzung dieser Kirche, und nur sie besitzt alle Wesenselemente dieser Kirche. Gleichzeitig gilt jedoch: „Nach katholischer Lehre kann man zu Recht sagen, dass die Kirche Christi in den Kirchen und kirchlichen Gemeinschaften, die noch nicht in voller Gemeinschaft mit der katholischen Kirche stehen, gegenwärtig und wirksam ist.“”

Translation: “The use of ‘subsists in’ instead of ‘is’ sparked broad discussion after the Council. In 2007 the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith published a document with several dubia on this topic. To the first question, ‘Did the Second Vatican Council change [mutare] the Catholic doctrine on the Church?’ the Congregation replied: The Second Vatican Council neither changed this doctrine nor intended to change it, but rather developed, deepened, and more fully explained it (Responses to Some Questions Regarding Certain Aspects of the Doctrine on the Church, 1).

Here the Congregation indicates that the Council did not make a fundamental change in doctrine. Nevertheless - to say it openly - through development, deepening, and fuller explanation, a change of a certain kind did take place. To the question, ‘What does it mean that the Church of Christ subsists in the Catholic Church?’ the Congregation replied: ‘Subsistence’ means this enduring historical continuity and the permanence of all the elements instituted by Christ in the Catholic Church, in which the Church of Christ is concretely found on this earth. According to Catholic doctrine, it can rightly be said that the Church of Christ is present and operative in the Churches and ecclesial Communities not yet in full communion with the Catholic Church, because of the elements of sanctification and truth present in them. Nevertheless, the word ‘subsists’ can be attributed to the Catholic Church alone because it refers to the mark of unity that we profess in the Creed (I believe … in the one Church); and this ‘one’ Church subsists in the Catholic Church (ibid., 2).

Finally, to the question, ‘Why was the expression “subsists in” adopted instead of the simple word “is”?’ it says: It comes from and more clearly expresses the fact that there are ‘numerous elements of sanctification and of truth’ which are found outside her structure, but which, ‘as gifts properly belonging to the Church of Christ, impel toward Catholic unity’ (ibid., 3).

Thus, by using ‘subsists in,’ the Council wanted to express a more complex relationship between the Church of Christ and the Catholic Church than the simple ‘is’ would have conveyed. In this way, a doctrinal development took place. In contrast to the earlier development - in which the Council spoke of ‘full incorporation’ rather than ‘membership’ and thus left the word ‘member’ usable in its traditional sense - here the Council makes a change that touches the core content of an existing doctrine. It is no longer possible (except colloquially, where ‘subsists’ would not be understood) to make so simple an identification of the Church of Christ with the Catholic Church. The core of the earlier teaching remains: Christ founded only one Church on earth, the Catholic Church is the organic continuation of that Church, and only she possesses all the essential elements of that Church. At the same time, however, it also holds that ‘according to Catholic doctrine, it can rightly be said that the Church of Christ is present and operative in the Churches and ecclesial Communities not yet in full communion with the Catholic Church.’”

The problem is that this new understanding, even if Cardinal Ratzinger tries not to admit it, leads to the conclusion that there are two churches, namely the Church of Christ and the Catholic Church. Since subsistit designates a real ontological relation, it presupposes numerically distinct relata, which means that de facto two churches are being spoken of, regardless of how close their relationship is described. That it is a real departure is admitted by Ratzinger himself when he writes in Deus locutus est nobis in Filio: Some Reflections on Subjectivity, Christology and the Church:[25]

“At this point, it is important to examine the word subsistit a bit more closely. With this term, the Council explicated the formula of Pope Pius XII, who had stated in his Encyclical Letter Mystici Corporis Christi that the Catholic Church “is” (est) the one mystical body of Christ. In the distinction between subsistit and est is hidden the entire ecumenical problem.”

Ratzinger thus admits here that there is a significant difference between est and subsistit. If the understanding of the relationship of the Church of Christ to the Catholic Church in LG is therefore not a clear equivalence, what is it? First, one can say that it is not relativism that we are accusing V2 of here. The explanatory documents on V2 show that it is by no means implied that the Church of Christ can also subsist in other churches besides the Catholic Church. Subsistence is in principle a unique realization. Ratzinger answers the liberal theologian Leonardo Boff:[26]

“The Council had instead chosen the word “subsistit” precisely to clarify that there is only one “subsistence” of the true Church, while outside its visible structure there exist only “elementa Ecclesiae” which - being elements of the same Church - tend toward and lead to the Catholic Church”

Here Ratzinger contradicts the relativistic understanding of LG, but nevertheless underlines that the Church of Christ extends beyond the Catholic Church and that elements of the Church of Christ are also found in other religions. That baptism and other valid sacraments can also be administered by heretics and schismatics was, of course, the teaching of the Church long before V2. This truth about elements of the Catholic Church outside her visible boundaries, however, could be formulated without introducing a distinction between the Church of Christ and the Catholic Church. One danger of this distinction can be that the Catholic Church is reduced to her visible part, as though the Church of Christ were the Mystical Body of Christ and its visible earthly institution were the Catholic Church. This understanding is suggested by the previously quoted formulation:

Original: “der katholischen Kirche, in der die Kirche Christi auf dieser Erde konkret anzutreffen ist.”

Translation: “the Catholic Church, in which the Church of Christ is concretely found on this earth.”

This view is supported by UR, where it says in 3:[27]

Original: “Denn wer an Christus glaubt und in der rechten Weise die Taufe empfangen hat, steht dadurch in einer gewissen, wenn auch nicht vollkommenen Gemeinschaft mit der katholischen Kirche. […] Nichtsdestoweniger sind sie durch den Glauben in der Taufe gerechtfertigt und Christus eingegliedert”

Translation: “For whoever believes in Christ and has received baptism in the right manner is thereby in a certain, though not perfect, communion with the Catholic Church. […] Nevertheless, through faith in baptism they are justified and incorporated into Christ.”

In the Catechism of the Catholic Church (CCC) 818, instead of “incorporated,” it directly says “made part of.” The prima facie implication of the text would therefore be that one can be part of the Body of Christ while standing only in imperfect communion with the Catholic Church, which again underlines the danger of believing that the Catholic Church is not the Body of Christ. That one becomes part of the Body of Christ through baptism is of course undisputed traditional doctrine, but previously one would have spoken here of an imperfect belonging to the Catholic Church and, by equivalence, of an imperfect belonging to the Mystical Body of Christ. The V2 formulations now suggest speaking of an imperfect, or even no, belonging to the Catholic Church, but of a perfect belonging to the Mystical Body / the Church of Christ. It should be noted that I am not claiming that since V2 there has been a unified doctrine of distinction between the Catholic Church and the Mystical Body of Christ. There are also quotations such as in Dominus Iesus 16:[28]

Original: “Wie es nur einen einzigen Christus gibt, so gibt es nur einen einzigen Leib Christi, eine einzige Braut Christi: »die eine alleinige katholische und apostolische Kirche«.”

Translation: “Just as there is only one Christ, so there is only one Body of Christ, one Bride of Christ: ‘the one and only Catholic and apostolic Church.’”

The argument is rather that once the equivalence is abandoned, absurd questions arise, such as whether the Church of Christ now subsists in the Mystical Body of Christ, or whether the Mystical Body of Christ subsists in the Catholic Church. Another danger of the distinction is that it regards the Church as divisible. As if, in the Great Schism, the Eastern “Orthodox” had not separated themselves from the Church, but had split the Church. This becomes clear in Ratzinger when he writes in Deus locutus est nobis in Filio: Some Reflections on Subjectivity, Christology and the Church:[29]

“But the difference between subsistit and est also embraces the drama of ecclesial division, for while the Church is only one and really exists, there is being which is from the Church’s being - there is ecclesial reality - outside the Church. Because sin is contradiction, the difference between subsistit and est cannot, in the final analysis, be completely resolved logically.”

Ratzinger therefore sees the est equivalence as an ideal case that we have fractured through the sin of schism. This leads Ratzinger to regard Eastern “Orthodox” schismatic sects as “true particular churches” (Deus locutus est nobis in Filio 17). This stands in direct contradiction to the teaching of Pius XI in Mortalium animos, where he writes:[30]

“For since the mystical body of Christ, in the same manner as His physical body, is one, compacted and fitly joined together, it were foolish and out of place to say that the mystical body is made up of members which are disunited and scattered abroad: whosoever therefore is not united with the body is no member of it, neither is he in communion with Christ its head.”

It remains to be noted here that the resistance to LG VIII follows from its contradiction with traditional teaching. The two dangers shown only underline the importance of resisting such a break with Tradition. In effect, LG VIII, by speaking of a real-ontological relationship between the Church of Christ and the Catholic Church, sets up the following dilemma: Either “Church” is used univocally - then subsistit, as a real relation, requires distinct entities, and there are therefore two churches, which contradicts Tradition. Or “Church” is used equivocally - then LG implicitly imports a Protestantizing separation of invisible and visible church, which would be even more foreign to Tradition.[31]

4.3. Justification of the Contradiction

After having presented some examples in which the FSSPX contradicts the documents of V2, we must now clarify whether resistance to them is justified. After all, resistance to V2 is cited as the point preventing reconciliation with Rome. First it should be noted that none of the controversial teachings in V2 was proclaimed as infallible. We have already discussed above that in this case public contradiction is justified under certain circumstances. This already follows from the fact that the Church cannot in principle “force” anyone to accept a statement whose truth content is in principle knowable, when the Church does not infallibly know that it is true.[32] But we have also discussed more precisely that the teaching of the Church since V2 also allows this. We have further seen that the traditional guidelines in Ott set far lower hurdles than the development since V2. But should the FSSPX therefore make use of this possibility, or would it not be their task as good Catholics to do everything possible to bring these statements somehow into harmony with Tradition? The first problem is that, for example in LG VIII, we saw how Ratzinger tried everything to do precisely that, but we nevertheless ended up with an insurmountable dilemma. The FSSPX recognizes by far the largest part of V2. In 90% of cases, V2 is completely in line with Tradition, which in the current Church crisis means that the FSSPX de facto agrees with more of V2 than many of the heads of the Church in regular offices. This statement can certainly be made with respect to the German bishops. Nor does the FSSPX know a black-and-white distinction between traditional teaching in V2 and Modernist heresy. Lefebvre himself spoke of a third category of texts that could be interpreted orthodoxly:[33]

“There are some conciliar documents that are obviously in conformity with Tradition […] Then there are some ambiguous texts, which nevertheless can somehow be “interpreted” correctly according to the previous Magisterium. But there are also some texts that are plainly in contradiction with Tradition and which can in no way be “integrated” with it”

In LG VIII we have already shown the clear dilemma and also shown the dangers to which such a formulation can lead. Without referring to a particular text, we have also already shown, using the example of William Lane Craig, how V2 has worked outwardly and how this new understanding of ecumenism from V2 seriously endangers the salvation of people outside the Church. Does that not justify, or even require, a refusal to sign the texts? Let us also recall once more what the situation was like in the Arian crisis. There it was not even about individual texts, nor individual sentences, nor even individual words, but only about a single letter. At that time one could surely have interpreted “God-like” in an orthodox way even more easily than some conciliar texts: “God-like precisely does not mean that Christ is not equal in essence to God the Father. This formulation was used precisely to make clear that Jesus is equal to the Father in His divine nature, yet nevertheless differs with respect to His essence insofar as He has two natures.” A misunderstood hermeneutic of continuity would then have led even more people into false belief. In the Arian controversy, as in selected conciliar texts, we see analogously how a text is false in its natural reading and how it is dangerous. Even if we presume good intentions on the part of the authors, that is not sufficient. In the year 635, Pope Honorius, in a letter that today would be classified as ordinary magisterium, supported the heresy of Monothelitism. Honorius wrote:[34]

Original: “Daher bekennen wir auch einen einzigen Willen unseres Herrn Jesus Christus […]”

Translation: “Therefore we also confess one single will of our Lord Jesus Christ […]”

The interesting thing is that Saint Maximus, who fought against Monothelitism, defended Honorius. He argued that Honorius had been misunderstood and had merely wanted to say that there were no contradictory wills in Jesus.[35] He therefore interpreted good intentions into the heretical statement, but that in no way led Maximus ever to sign such a statement himself. This is precisely the situation in which the FSSPX finds itself. Even if one can certainly accuse it at times of not always presuming the best intentions, this historical example shows how a good intention does not negate the statement of a text. Whoever compulsively tries to interpret all V2 texts, which can indeed contain errors, orthodoxly places himself in extreme danger of falling into semantic relativism. This is a very great and little-discussed danger.

4.4. Heresies from Vatican II

LG 16 could also be counted among the non-harmonizable passages.[36] But I also want to show the danger of the harmonizable passages. It is not ecclesiastical practice to condemn only “non-harmonizable” statements. We have already seen how this was not the case with the Arian formulas. The question that must always be asked is, “What does the text as such, in its context, intend to express?” From this perspective, statements that are not strictly heretical must also be condemned. To sign them would mean the sin of scandal, which we will address later in connection with the Mass. The aim of this paragraph is to show how heretical statements were justified by V2. This serves not only to justify resistance to V2, but also once more to substantiate the fact of a severe Church crisis.

4.4.1. Continuance of the Old Covenant

On the continuance of the Old Covenant, Cantate Domino says:[37]

“It firmly believes, professes and teaches that the legal prescriptions of the old Testament or the Mosaic law, which are divided into ceremonies, holy sacrifices and sacraments, because they were instituted to signify something in the future, although they were adequate for the divine cult of that age, once our lord Jesus Christ who was signified by them had come, came to an end and the sacraments of the new Testament had their beginning. Whoever, after the passion, places his hope in the legal prescriptions and submits himself to them as necessary for salvation and as if faith in Christ without them could not save, sins mortally. It does not deny that from Christ’s passion until the promulgation of the gospel they could have been retained, provided they were in no way believed to be necessary for salvation. But it asserts that after the promulgation of the gospel they cannot be observed without loss of eternal salvation. Therefore it denounces all who after that time observe circumcision, the sabbath and other legal prescriptions as strangers to the faith of Christ and unable to share in eternal salvation, unless they recoil at some time from these errors. Therefore it strictly orders all who glory in the name of Christian, not to practice circumcision either before or after baptism, since whether or not they place their hope in it, it cannot possibly be observed without loss of eternal salvation.”

Justin Martyr wrote:[38]

“For the law promulgated on Horeb is now old, and belongs to yourselves alone; but this is for all universally. Now, law placed against law has abrogated that which is before it, and a covenant which comes after in like manner has put an end to the previous one.”

Thomas Aquinas wrote on this:[39]

“Just as it would be a mortal sin now for anyone, in making a profession of faith, to say that Christ is yet to be born, which the fathers of old said devoutly and truthfully; so too it would be a mortal sin now to observe those ceremonies which the fathers of old fulfilled with devotion and fidelity.”

In contrast to this traditional teaching that the Old Covenant has been replaced, V2 says in NA 4:[40]

Original: “Nichtsdestoweniger sind die Juden nach dem Zeugnis der Apostel immer noch von Gott geliebt um der Väter willen; sind doch seine Gnadengaben und seine Berufung unwiderruflich.” […] Gewiß ist die Kirche das neue Volk Gottes, trotzdem darf man die Juden nicht als von Gott verworfen oder verflucht darstellen, als wäre dies aus der Heiligen Schrift zu folgern.”

Translation: “Nevertheless, according to the testimony of the apostles, the Jews are still beloved by God for the sake of the fathers; for His gifts and His calling are irrevocable.” […] “Certainly the Church is the new People of God; nevertheless the Jews must not be presented as rejected by God or cursed, as though this followed from Sacred Scripture.”

This statement is not in direct contradiction to supersessionism, but the statements that followed it certainly were. Thus John Paul II said:[41]

Original: “Die erste Dimension dieses Dialogs, nämlich die Begegnung zwischen dem Gottesvolk des von Gott nie gekündigten Alten Bundes und dem des Neuen Bundes, ist zugleich ein Dialog innerhalb unserer Kirche, gleichsam zwischen dem ersten und zweiten Teil ihrer Bibel.”

Translation: “The first dimension of this dialogue, namely the encounter between the People of God of the Old Covenant never revoked by God and that of the New Covenant, is at the same time a dialogue within our Church, as it were between the first and second parts of her Bible.”

This statement eventually found its way into the Catechism of the Catholic Church.[42] That this statement is false against supersessionism was indirectly admitted by Benedict XVI when he tried to qualify the statement:[43]

Original: “An der Formulierung vom «nie gekündigtem Bund» sei „richtig, dass es keine Kündigung von seiten Gottes gibt.“”

Translation: “What is correct in the formulation of the ‘never revoked covenant’ is that ‘there is no revocation on God’s part.’”

4.4.2. Ecumenism of Return

That V2 teaches a false ecumenism cannot be shown by a single quotation; it is rather the obvious conclusion from several statements. In Unitatis redintegratio (UR) 3 it says, among other things:

Original: “Denn der Geist Christi hat es nicht abgelehnt, sie als Mittel des Heiles zu gebrauchen, deren Kraft aus der Fülle der Gnade und Wahrheit stammt, die der katholischen Kirche anvertraut ist.”

Translation: “For the Spirit of Christ has not refused to use them as means of salvation, whose power derives from the fullness of grace and truth entrusted to the Catholic Church.”

and[44]

Original: “Daher sind diese getrennten Kirchen und Gemeinschaften, obwohl wir glauben, dass sie Mängel aufweisen, keineswegs ohne Bedeutung und Gewicht im Mysterium des Heiles.”

Translation: “Therefore these separated Churches and Communities, although we believe that they suffer from defects, are by no means without significance and weight in the mystery of salvation.”

Relevant here, of course, is also the subsistit that we analyzed above. Even if one interprets these statements benevolently, it is evident that they have led to clearly heretical statements. Thus Benedict XVI said at World Youth Day 2005:[45]

“And we now ask: What does it mean to restore the unity of all Christians?… This unity, we are convinced, indeed subsists in the Catholic Church, without the possibility of ever being lost (Unitatis Redintegratio, nn. 2, 4, etc.); the Church in fact has not totally disappeared from the world. On the other hand, this unity does not mean what could be called ecumenism of the return: that is, to deny and to reject one’s own faith history. Absolutely not!”

This stands in direct contradiction to the understanding of Pius XI on ecumenism, who writes:[46]

“the union of Christians can only be promoted by promoting the return to the one true Church of Christ of those who are separated from it”

John Paul II also contradicted correct ecumenism when he signed the Balamand Statement, in which it said:[47]

“14. It is in this perspective that the Catholic Churches and the Orthodox Churches recognize each other as Sister Churches, responsible together for maintaining the Church of God in fidelity to the divine purpose, most especially in what concerns unity. According to the words of Pope John Paul II, the ecumenical endeavour of the Sister Churches of East and West, grounded in dialogue and prayer, is the search for perfect and total communion which is neither absorption nor fusion but a meeting in truth and love (cf. Slavorum Apostoli, n. 27). 15.While the inviolable freedom of persons and their obligation to follow the requirements of their conscience remain secure, in the search for re-establishing unity there is no question of conversion of people from one Church to the other in order to ensure their salvation. There is a question of achieving together the will of Christ for his own and the design of God for his Church by means of a common quest by the Churches for a full accord on the content of the faith and its implications.”

Pope Francis drove this to its peak by repeatedly condemning proselytism, for example:[48]

Original: “Als ich aus der Sakristei kam, stand da eine sehr elegante Dame mit einem Jungen und einem Mädchen neben sich und sagte mir: ‚Padre, ich bin zufrieden, denn ich habe diese beiden hier bekehrt‘. Da bin ich ärgerlich geworden und habe geantwortet: ‚Du hast überhaupt niemanden bekehrt, du hast es an Respekt für diese beiden fehlen lassen! Du hast sie nicht begleitet, sondern Proselytismus betrieben, aber das ist keine Evangelisierung.‘ Die war stolz, weil sie jemanden bekehrt hatte! Achtet darauf, gut zu unterscheiden zwischen dem apostolischen Handeln und dem Proselytismus: Wir betreiben keinen Proselytismus; der Herr hat nie Proselytismus betrieben.”

Translation: “When I came out of the sacristy, there stood a very elegant lady with a boy and a girl beside her, and she said to me: ‘Padre, I am pleased, because I converted these two here.’ I became angry and answered: ‘You did not convert anyone at all; you failed to show respect for these two! You did not accompany them, but practiced proselytism, and that is not evangelization.’ She was proud because she had converted someone! Be careful to distinguish well between apostolic action and proselytism: we do not practice proselytism; the Lord never practiced proselytism.”

4.4.3. Non-Catholic Services

On non-Catholic services, UR 8 says:[49]

Original: “Bei besonderen Anlässen, zum Beispiel bei Gebeten, die”für die Einheit” verrichtet werden, und bei ökumenischen Versammlungen, ist es erlaubt und auch erwünscht, daß sich die Katholiken mit den getrennten Brüdern im Gebet zusammenfinden. Solche gemeinsamen Gebete sind ein höchst wirksames Mittel, um die Gnade der Einheit zu erflehen, und ein echter Ausdruck der Gemeinsamkeit, in der die Katholiken mit den getrennten Brüdern immer noch verbunden sind: “Denn wo zwei oder drei versammelt sind in meinem Namen, da bin ich mitten unter ihnen”

Translation: “On special occasions, for example in prayers offered ‘for unity’ and at ecumenical gatherings, it is permitted and even desirable that Catholics join with the separated brethren in prayer. Such common prayers are a most effective means of imploring the grace of unity and a genuine expression of the communion in which Catholics are still joined with the separated brethren: ‘For where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I in the midst of them.’”

This first stands opposed to the statement of Mortalium animos (MA):[46]

“So, Venerable Brethren, it is clear why this Apostolic See has never allowed its subjects to take part in the assemblies of non-Catholics: for the union of Christians can only be promoted by promoting the return to the one true Church of Christ of those who are separated from it, for in the past they have unhappily left it”

as well as to the 1917 Code of Canon Law:[50]

“§ 1. It is not licit for the faithful by any manner to assist actively or to have a part in the sacred [rites] of non-Catholics. § 2. Passive or merely material presence can be tolerated for the sake of honor or civil office,for grave reason approved by the Bishop in case of doubt, at the funerals, weddings, and similarsolemnities of non-Catholics, provided danger of perversion and scandal is absent.”

Here too one can again argue that the statement is harmonizable, because the statement could, for example, refer only to passive participation. Nevertheless, this statement has led to clearly false actions. The prayer meeting at Assisi is, of course, the classic example. But the most glaring example is probably Archbishop Flavio Pace. After the Anglican “bishop” Sarah Mullally had been permitted to celebrate a “Mass” in St. Peter’s Basilica, Pace made the sign of the cross when Mullally gave a “blessing.”[51]

He received no disciplinary consequences for this.

4.4.4. Conclusion

Even if the statements from V2 named here may not be direct contradictions, the examples in this paragraph should at least show once more how grave the Church crisis is and how little the faithful can rely on receiving the true doctrine of the Catholic Church from their shepherds. While the texts of V2 must be read according to what they intend to say in their context, the words and actions of their representatives must be read benevolently in caritas. It is not a contradiction to read the best possible intentions into the statements and actions of bishops and popes while nevertheless clearly rejecting them as objectively false and scandalous. That is precisely the boundary the FSSPX draws, in contrast to sedevacantists on one side and in contrast to other conservative groups on the other.

5. The Novus Ordo

After the refusal to sign all statements of V2, the refusal to celebrate the Novus Ordo (NO) is the second point where the FSSPX offers resistance. Here too it must be examined whether this is justified. For this coming section I must first disclose that my argument, although it is fundamentally the same as that of the FSSPX, will differ from their argument insofar as I have a different attitude toward the NO.

5.1. Is the NO Bad in Itself?

Within the FSSPX there is an internal discussion about whether the NO should be called “intrinsically evil.” From my perspective this is a category mistake. Only actions can be evil, so at most one could say that attending or celebrating the NO is intrinsically evil, not the Mass itself. The FSSPX itself does not de facto maintain the former, since it explicitly permits the faithful, albeit only passively, to attend NO Masses under certain conditions, such as the funeral or wedding of close relatives. On the other hand, the FSSPX goes so far as to advise the faithful against attending the NO even if they can find no other Mass on a Sunday. This is the point at which I do not agree with the FSSPX. Yet this is not a disagreement in principle. The FSSPX would argue that attending the NO constitutes a real danger to the faith, among other things to faith in transubstantiation. I do believe that this danger exists broadly speaking, but not that there is a real danger for a well-informed traditional Catholic, especially if he does not usually attend the NO but, for example, only does so while on vacation. Leaving aside the discussion about “intrinsically evil,” the FSSPX would certainly say that the NO is “bad in itself.” What it means by this is something like the claim that there can be no good New Mass, or in a weakened form - to which I would also agree - that a good NO is a bad Mass, and a good Mass is at most a bad NO, in the sense that it implements the NO unintentionally. Father Schmidberger uses the analogy of a catechism that does not describe the Trinity with a single word. This catechism would not be bad because it necessarily teaches something false, but because it omits something essential. Father Gaudron develops this argument in “Ist der Novus Ordo Missae in sich schlecht?” and summarizes the core argument as follows:[52]

Original: “Er ist schlecht, weil er 1.) den Opfercharakter der Messe und die Transsubstantiation verdunkelt. Ein Meßritus hat aber wesentlich die Aufgabe, das Wesen der hl. Messe zu verdeutlichen und zum Ausdruck zu bringen und nicht, dieses Wesen zu verschleiern. Der NOM erfüllt also eine geschuldete Aufgabe nicht und ist schon von daher in sich schlecht. Er ist aber auch 2.) schlecht, weil er das Wesen der Messe nicht nur verdunkelt, sondern sogar eine falsche Auffassung von der Messe nahelegt, nämlich diejenige eines brüderlichen Mahls der Gemeinde, bei dem das Gedächtnis an das letzte Abendmahl Jesu mit seinen Aposteln begangen wird. Ein Priester, der das Problem des NOM nicht erkannt hat oder nicht genügend durchschaut, kann daher zwar von persönlicher Schuld ganz frei sein, wenn er die neue Messe liest, objektiv ist es aber schlecht, daß er diesen Ritus verwendet.”

Translation: “It is bad because 1.) it obscures the sacrificial character of the Mass and transubstantiation. But a Mass rite essentially has the task of making clear and expressing the essence of the Holy Mass, and not of concealing this essence. The NOM therefore fails to fulfill an owed task and for this reason alone is bad in itself. But it is also 2.) bad because it not only obscures the essence of the Mass but even suggests a false conception of the Mass, namely that of a fraternal meal of the congregation at which the memorial of Jesus’ Last Supper with His apostles is celebrated. A priest who has not recognized the problem of the NOM or has not sufficiently understood it may therefore be entirely free from personal guilt when he says the New Mass, but objectively it is bad that he uses this rite.”

The argument can therefore be formalized as follows:

  1. Something is bad in itself if it fails to fulfill one of its essential tasks.
  2. Making clear and not obscuring the sacrificial character of the Mass is an essential task of the Mass.
  3. The NO obscures the sacrificial character of the Mass and does not make it clear.
  4. Therefore the NO is bad in itself.

We now want to examine whether all three premises are correct.

5.1.1. Premise: Something Is Bad in Itself If It Fails to Fulfill One of Its Essential Tasks

The first premise should be relatively uncontroversial insofar as it follows directly from the Thomistic metaphysics of evil as a privation of good. Saint Thomas writes:[53][54]

“But not every absence of good is evil. For absence of good can be taken in a privative and in a negative sense. Absence of good, taken negatively, is not evil; otherwise, it would follow that what does not exist is evil, and also that everything would be evil, through not having the good belonging to something else; for instance, a man would be evil who had not the swiftness of the roe, or the strength of a lion. But the absence of good, taken in a privative sense, is an evil; as, for instance, the privation of sight is called blindness.” “Because evil is the privation of good, and not a mere negation, as was said above (Article 3), therefore not every defect of good is an evil, but the defect of the good which is naturally due. For the want of sight is not an evil in a stone, but it is an evil in an animal; since it is against the nature of a stone to see.”

5.1.2. Premise: Making Clear and Not Obscuring the Sacrificial Character of the Mass Is an Essential Task of the Mass

This premise is less obviously true, but it is supported by several statements of the Magisterium. Thus, for example, Pius XII writes in Mediator Dei (MD):[55]

Original: “Der Kult, der von ihr Gott dem Herrn erwiesen wird, ist, wie Augustinus kurz und treffend sagt, ein fortgesetztes Bekenntnis des katholischen Glaubens und eine Übung der Hoffnung und Liebe: „Durch Glaube, Hoffnung und Liebe - so erklärt er - ist Gott zu verehren“. In der heiligen Liturgie bekennen wir den katholischen Glauben ausdrücklich und offen nicht nur durch die Feier der Geheimnisse, die Darbringung des heiligen Opfers und die Spendung der Sakramente, sondern ebenso durch das Beten oder Singen des Glaubensbekenntnisses, welches das Kennzeichen oder sozusagen der Ausweis der Gläubigen ist, sowie durch die Lesung anderer Texte und besonders der unter Eingebung des Heiligen Geistes aufgezeichneten Heiligen Schrift. Die Liturgie als Ganzes enthält daher den katholischen Glauben, insofern sie den Glauben der Kirche öffentlich bezeugt.”

Translation: “The worship which she renders to God the Lord is, as Augustine says briefly and aptly, a continued profession of the Catholic faith and an exercise of hope and charity: ‘Through faith, hope, and charity,’ he explains, ‘God is to be worshipped.’ In the sacred liturgy we profess the Catholic faith expressly and openly, not only through the celebration of the mysteries, the offering of the holy sacrifice, and the administration of the sacraments, but likewise through the praying or singing of the Creed, which is the mark or, so to speak, the identification of the faithful, as well as through the reading of other texts and especially of Sacred Scripture written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit. The liturgy as a whole therefore contains the Catholic faith insofar as it publicly bears witness to the faith of the Church.”

But the premise is confirmed more explicitly at the Council of Trent, where it says:[56]

“His priesthood might not come to an end with His death,[2] at the last supper, on the night He was betrayed, that He might leave to His beloved spouse the Church a visible sacrifice, such as the nature of man requires, whereby that bloody sacrifice once to be accomplished on the cross might be represented […]” “And since the nature of man is such that he cannot without external means be raised easily to meditation on divine things, holy mother Church has instituted certain rites, namely, that some things in the mass be pronounced in a low tone and others in a louder tone. She has likewise, in accordance with apostolic discipline and tradition, made use of ceremonies, such as mystical blessings, lights, incense, vestments, and many other things of this kind, whereby both the majesty of so great a sacrifice might be emphasized and the minds of the faithful excited by those visible signs of religion and piety to the contemplation of those most sublime things which are hidden in this sacrifice.”

Trent therefore says: the rite has the task of representing the sacrifice, and this is constitutive, not accidental, because human nature requires it.

5.1.3. Premise: The NO Obscures the Sacrificial Character of the Mass and Does Not Make It Clear

Now we come to the truly controversial premise of the argument. Does the NO really obscure the sacrificial character of the Mass? After all, in the NO the very same words of consecration are spoken. Before the consecration, the Suscipiat Dominus is also still prayed in the NO, where it explicitly says:

Original: “Der Herr nehme das Opfer an aus deinen Händen zum Lob und Ruhm seines Namens, zum Segen für uns und seine ganze heilige Kirche.”

Translation: “May the Lord accept the sacrifice from your hands for the praise and glory of His name, for our good and the good of all His holy Church.”

How, then, can one say that the NO obscures the sacrifice? The real problem lies in the fact that it is difficult to draw an exact boundary. A Catholic catechism must mention the Trinity. But must it mention every doctrine? If it must mention every doctrine, how explicitly must it mention it? Must it explain it in detail and anticipate every ambiguous interpretive possibility? This cannot be clearly defined. But just because one cannot define from how many or how long stubbles a beard begins, it does not follow that one cannot distinguish between a full beard and a fresh shave. Therefore we now want to look more closely at the changes to the Mass. Why should one believe that the NO obscures the sacrificial character? The first reason to believe this is that this was the intention of some council fathers. Father Gaudron writes:[57]

Original: “A. Bugnini, gab offen zu, daß er aus der Messe alles entfernen wollte, was für die „getrennten Brüdern“ auch nur ein Schatten eines Steins des Anstoßes darstellen könnte”

Translation: “A. Bugnini openly admitted that he wanted to remove from the Mass everything that could represent even the shadow of a stumbling block for the ‘separated brethren.’”

The sacrifice is precisely the main stumbling block. Thus Luther wrote:

Original: „Zum achten folget der ganze Greuel, dem alles dienen hat müssen, was in der Messe vorher gangen ist, darum es auch Offertorium genannt, das ist ein Opfergesang. Hernach, was dem folget, klingt und stinkt allzumal eitel Opfer und sind die heiligen Wort des Lebens und Seligkeit (d.h. der Einsetzungsbericht H.B.M.) mitten unter das greuich Opferwesen dermaß hineingesetzt wie vor Zeiten die Archa des Herm (d.h. die Bundeslade W.G.) im abgöttischen Tempel neben dem Götzen Dagon…. Darum wollen wir alles unterlassen, so nach Opfer klinget samt dem ganzen Kanon und allein behalten, das rein und heilig ist, und unser Messe also anfahen.“

Translation: “Eighth follows the whole abomination, to which everything that came before in the Mass had to serve; for this reason it is also called the Offertory, that is, a sacrificial chant. What follows after that sounds and stinks altogether of sheer sacrifice, and the holy words of life and salvation (that is, the institution narrative) are placed in the midst of the gruesome sacrificial apparatus just as formerly the Ark of the Lord (that is, the Ark of the Covenant) was placed in the idolatrous temple beside the idol Dagon…. Therefore we want to omit everything that sounds like sacrifice, together with the whole canon, and retain only what is pure and holy, and begin our Mass thus.”

(quoted in [52], p. 7) Gaudron also quotes Jean Guitton, who said in an interview:[52]

Original: “Paul VI. hat alles in seiner Macht Stehende getan, um die katholische Messe - über das Konzil von Trient hinweg - dem protestantischen Abendmahl anzunähern…. Es gibt bei Paul VI. eine ökumenische Absicht, all das, was es in der Messe an allzu Katholischem im traditionellen Sinn gibt, auszulöschen oder wenigstens zu korrigieren, oder wenigstens abzumildern, um die katholische Messe, ich wiederhole es, der kalvinistischen Messe anzunähern”

Translation: “Paul VI did everything in his power to bring the Catholic Mass - beyond the Council of Trent - closer to the Protestant Lord’s Supper…. In Paul VI there is an ecumenical intention to erase, or at least correct, or at least soften, everything in the Mass that is too Catholic in the traditional sense, in order to bring the Catholic Mass, I repeat, closer to the Calvinist Mass.”

The obscuring of the sacrifice was therefore a goal of at least some council fathers. The question remains whether they succeeded. Before that, however, we should mention another indicator suggesting that the NO obscures the sacrificial character, namely the original version of the Institutio Generalis Missalis Romani (IGMR), from which Gaudron quotes:

Original: “Der dem neuen Meßbuch vorangestellte § 7 gibt dann auch eine ganz protestantische Definition der Messe: „Das Herrenmahl oder die Messe ist die hl. Zusammenkunft oder die Versammlung des Volkes Gottes, das unter dem Vorsitz eines Priesters zusammenkommt, um das Gedächtnis des Herrn zu feiern. Deshalb gilt von der örtlichen Versammlung der heiligen Kirche in hervorragendem Maß die Verheißung Christi: Wo zwei oder drei in meinem Namen versammelt sind, da bin ich mitten unter ihnen.“ Diese Worte passen vollkommen auf eine protestantische Abendmahlsfeier, nicht aber auf eine katholische Messe. Sie geben jedoch gut den Geist wieder, der die Schöpfer des NOM beseelte.”

Translation: “Paragraph 7 prefixed to the new Missal therefore also gives a thoroughly Protestant definition of the Mass: ‘The Lord’s Supper or the Mass is the sacred assembly or gathering of the People of God, which comes together under the presidency of a priest to celebrate the memorial of the Lord. Therefore the promise of Christ applies in an eminent way to the local assembly of the holy Church: where two or three are gathered in my name, there am I in the midst of them.’ These words fit perfectly a Protestant Lord’s Supper service, but not a Catholic Mass. Yet they accurately reflect the spirit that animated the creators of the NOM.”

It must be noted here that this clearly heretical formulation was later softened into the current:[58]

Original: “In der Messe, dem Herrenmahl, wird das Volk Gottes zu einer Gemeinschaft unter dem Vorsitz des Priesters, der Christus in seinem Tun repräsentiert, zusammengerufen, um die Gedächtnisfeier des Herrn, das eucharistische Opfer, zu begehen”

Translation: “In the Mass, the Lord’s Supper, the People of God is called together into a community under the presidency of the priest, who represents Christ in his action, in order to celebrate the memorial of the Lord, the Eucharistic sacrifice.”

The formulation is no longer clearly heretical. But as Father Schmidberger remarked in a conversation with then-Cardinal Ratzinger, the formulation was changed, but the Mass itself never was. Before we continue, I want once again to stress how scandalous it is that the Church today celebrates a Mass which, according to its own explanatory document, proceeds from a clearly heretical understanding of the Mass. After the examples so far, we can now equate the question “Does the NO obscure the sacrificial character?” with “Were the council fathers around Bugnini successful in their goal?” or with “Does the first version of the IGMR accurately describe the NO?” Father Gaudron cites several examples for this:

  • The abolition of the Offertory, which expressed in a perfect way the propitiatory sacrificial character of the Mass, and its replacement by a preparation of the gifts modeled on a Jewish table prayer.
  • The quasi-abolition of the Roman Canon and its replacement by alternative canons that do not have the same dogmatic value. Especially of the second canon it was written that it “could be celebrated with complete peace of conscience by a priest who no longer believes either in transubstantiation or in the sacrificial character of the Mass, and that it would therefore also be perfectly suited for celebration by a Protestant minister.” Because of its brevity, however, this canon is precisely the one most often used.
  • The deletion of the priest’s first genuflection after the consecration. As small as this change may appear at first glance, it nevertheless accommodates the Protestant view that the consecration is effected not by the words of the priest but by the faith of the people. For that reason the priest genuflects only after he has shown the species to the people. Something similar applies to the replacement of the formula for distributing Communion, “Corpus Domini nostri Jesu Christi custodiat…,” with the simple “The Body of Christ.” For the Lutheran, the Real Presence is given only in usu, in reception, and is effected by faith.
  • The removal of many genuflections and most signs of blessing suppresses the character of the sacred and of the mystery of the Mass and promotes the understanding of it as a mere memorial celebration in which Christ is present only symbolically. The same applies to the use of the vernacular and the disappearance of the priest’s silent prayer.

Now one could say: “OK, these are examples of how the sacrificial character appears less in the NO than in the TLM, but there is still enough in it that it is sufficiently expressed.” But here one must ask: what is still there? Of course, the consecration, and with it the offering. But these are precisely the necessary elements for a Mass to come about at all. If these elements were not there, there would be no sacrificial character that could be obscured. In my opinion, the best argument of NO apologists would be the Suscipiat. Strictly speaking, this is not only my opinion. The Suscipiat was a thorn in the side of some council fathers:[59]

Original: “Für die Einstellung der liturgischen Reformatoren ist es bezeichnend, daß es zur Erhaltung dieses Gebetes eines päpstlichen Machtworts bedurfte. Zu sehr war und ist vielen wie den „Reformatoren“ des 16, Jahrhunderts die Rede vom Opfer ein Greuel. Nur die Deutung als Lobopfer, die auch den Protestanten möglich erscheint, konnte sie überhaupt mit diesem Text versöhnen. Dieses Gebet erscheint den liturgischen Revolutionären aber auch deshalb besonders anstößig, weil es eine der wenigen Stellen ist, an denen die Rolle des Priesters noch eigens Erwähnung findet, heißt es doch da; „Betet Brüder., daß mein und euer Opfer Gott… gefalle.“ Dieses „mein und euer“ ist deshalb in der erwähnten liturgischen Kommission „nicht unumstritten“. Deshalb soll das ganze Gebet nach deren Plänen „ersatzlos gestrichen“ werden. Statt dessen soll - ohne das Wort ‘Opfer’ - gebetet werden: „Lasset uns beten Allmächtigen Vater, daß er die Gaben der Kirche annehme zu seinem Lob und zum Heil der ganzen Welt“”

Translation: “It is characteristic of the attitude of the liturgical reformers that a papal command was needed to preserve this prayer. For many, as for the ‘reformers’ of the sixteenth century, talk of sacrifice was and remains too much of an abomination. Only the interpretation as a sacrifice of praise, which also appears possible to Protestants, could reconcile them with this text at all. This prayer also appears particularly objectionable to the liturgical revolutionaries because it is one of the few places where the role of the priest is still specifically mentioned, for it says: ‘Pray, brethren, that my sacrifice and yours may be acceptable to God….’ This ‘mine and yours’ is therefore ‘not undisputed’ in the liturgical commission mentioned. For that reason, according to their plans, the entire prayer was to be ‘deleted without replacement.’ Instead, without the word ‘sacrifice,’ the prayer was to be: ‘Let us pray to the almighty Father that He may accept the gifts of the Church for His praise and for the salvation of the whole world.’”

Has the sacrificial character therefore at least remained clearly outwardly visible in the Suscipiat in the NO? Unfortunately, not even really there. For in 390 of the IGMR it says:[60]

Original: “Es ist Sache der Bischofskonferenzen, die in dieser Grundordnung und im Ordo Missae angegebenen Anpassungen festzulegen und, nach deren Rekognoszierung durch den Apostolischen Stuhl, in das Messbuch einzuführen. Das sind: […] - Texte der Gesänge zur Eröffnung, zur Darbringung derGaben und zur Kommunion (vgl. oben, Nrn. 48, 74, 87);”

Translation: “It belongs to the conferences of bishops to establish the adaptations indicated in this General Instruction and in the Ordo Missae, and, after their recognition by the Apostolic See, to introduce them into the Missal. These are: […] - texts of the chants at the entrance, at the presentation of the gifts, and at Communion (cf. above, nos. 48, 74, 87);”

The German bishops, for example, made use of this freedom. In Die Feier der Gemeindemesse 87 there are now three options, and instead of the Suscipiat one may alternatively pray:[61]

Original: “Lasset uns beten zu Gott, dem allmächtigen Vater, daß er die Gaben der Kirche annehme zu seinem Lob und zum Heil der ganzen Welt.”

Translation: “Let us pray to God, the almighty Father, that He may accept the gifts of the Church for His praise and for the salvation of the whole world.”

Here again it is obvious how the sacrificial character is replaced by that of a meal.

5.1.4. Conclusion

We have now seen how Father Gaudron set up a valid argument from three premises and the conclusion “The NO is bad in itself.” We defended the first two relatively uncontroversial premises and showed that some arguments also support the third premise, from which it would follow that the NO is in fact bad in itself.

5.2. The Moderate Position

As mentioned at the beginning, my position is somewhat different from that of the FSSPX. More precisely, I see my position as the one the FSSPX should adopt, because it brings together all of its basic assumptions in a logically consistent way. I would agree that the NO is bad in itself insofar as it describes the Mass as a rite, but I would also hold that not every Mass celebrated according to the NO rite is a bad Mass. My analogy is that of a cookbook. A cookbook describes how a dish must be cooked, but it can also leave room for variation, and this room must remain within certain limits if the cookbook is to be good. If a cookbook allows the option of replacing salt with rat poison in its dishes, then it is a bad cookbook, because it leaves room outside the bounds of a good cookbook. However, this does not yet mean that all dishes prepared according to this cookbook are bad, since one can simply cook them with salt rather than rat poison. By this I am not saying that all problems with the NO are purely accidental; the NO clearly has Protestantizing tendencies, as Father Gaudron’s examples show. But these are not sufficient to say that the sacrificial character is obscured. Of a Mass such as at the Oxford Oratory, celebrated in Latin ad orientem, with Communion only on the tongue and without replacing the Suscipiat, one simply cannot say that the sacrificial character would not be clearly communicated.[62] Father Gaudron answers this:

Original: “Auch bei der konservativsten Form des NOM finden sich der Wegfall des Offertoriums, der Kniebeugen und Segenszeichen. Auch diese Form stellt ein „auffälliges Abrücken von der katholischen Theologie der Messe dar.“”

Translation: “Even in the most conservative form of the NOM one finds the disappearance of the Offertory, the genuflections, and the signs of blessing. This form too represents a ‘striking departure from the Catholic theology of the Mass.’”

He also gives further arguments against the best possible NO. The problem is that they are all insufficient. They may well be true, but for his argument to work he would have to show that even the best possible NO obscures the sacrificial character, and his arguments simply do not carry that far. If, however, one accepts my position that the NO is bad in itself but that the individual Mass can nevertheless be good, then all the pieces of the puzzle fit together. The first two uncontroversial premises remain the same; only the third is interpreted differently and can be defended much more easily. Instead of having to show that the NO obscures the sacrificial character, the burden of proof falls to showing only that the NO leaves too much room to obscure the sacrificial character. Now one can mention not only the essential deficiencies of the NO listed by Father Gaudron, but also accidental properties such as:

  • Communion in the hand
  • Extraordinary ministers of Communion
  • Ad populum
  • The possibility of replacing the Suscipiat

If the premise is changed from “The NO obscures the sacrificial character of the Mass and does not make it clear” to “The NO leaves too much room to obscure the sacrificial character of the Mass and not make it clear,” the premise becomes hardly controversial for any conservative Catholic. The second premise, “Making clear and not obscuring the sacrificial character of the Mass is an essential task of the Mass,” remains untouched, because the NO fails to fulfill this task simply by leaving this room. The first premise also remains untouched. The FSSPX could also continue to maintain its principle of advising the faithful against attending the NO, because the danger that the individual Mass is also bad could still be judged too high. Nor does my position really contradict the quotations from Bishop Lefebvre. For example, he says:

Original: “Die Sünde wird schwer, wenn eine informierte und gewarnte Person trotzdem regelmäßig dorthin geht und sagt: >Mir ist es egal, ich fürchte nicht um meinen Glauben<, obwohl sie genau weiß, dass es gefährlich ist”

Translation: “The sin becomes grave when an informed and warned person nevertheless goes there regularly and says, ‘I do not care; I do not fear for my faith,’ although he knows very well that it is dangerous.”

One can continue to agree with this. It is simply a case-by-case judgment whether the person really thinks that the NO is dangerous to his faith. The other quotation is the following:

Original: „Wer nicht zu diesen Messen gehen kann [gemeint ist die überlieferte Messe], soll sie sonntags in seinem Messbuch lesen, wenn möglich innerhalb der Familie, wie die Christen in den Missionsländern tun, die nur zwei- oder dreimal, manchmal nur einmal im Jahr vom Priester besucht werden.“

Translation: “Whoever cannot go to these Masses [meaning the traditional Mass] should read them on Sundays in his missal, if possible within the family, as Christians do in mission countries who are visited by a priest only two or three times, sometimes only once, a year.”

One can also still agree with this quotation if it is interpreted as general pastoral advice that allows exceptions in individual cases, just as the FSSPX already allows exceptions in individual cases, such as funerals of close relatives. The moderate position could be described with an adapted analogy of the catechism. We would no longer say, “A catechism is not only bad if it teaches falsehood, but also if it withholds something essential,” but would offer an analogy such as the following:

“A curriculum in a Catholic seminary that can be implemented correctly in such a way that the student ends up with a Protestant understanding of theology is a bad curriculum, even if the curriculum also makes possible the teaching of a good course.”

5.3. Does the Moderate Position Justify Resistance?

The attentive reader will now object that the moderate position lowers the burden of proof, but at the same time also lowers the gravity of the consequences that can be drawn from it. Let us take an atheist as an example. He would argue against any involvement of the Church in political matters, since from his perspective the basis for the Church’s position is grounded in a false belief. He might argue fanatically that the Church has nothing to say about the morality of laws because its positions are derived from irrational, outdated positions in a man-made Bible. An agnostic could now say, “Hey, you can simply say that you do not know whether God exists. That lowers your burden of proof. You no longer need to present negative arguments against God, but only undermine the arguments for God.” The problem, however, is that the agnostic then has a much weaker argument against the Church’s involvement in political questions. He can no longer invoke the claim that the Church’s positions are derived from irrational, outdated positions in a man-made Bible. He has to stay out of such claims because he does not claim to be able to answer such questions. At first glance, a similar dilemma follows from the moderate position on the NO: if the NO can also be celebrated in a way pleasing to God, how can one refuse to do it? The Church and the pope fundamentally have the right to change the liturgy. If they were to introduce a heretical Mass, one could of course resist it, but why also resist a rite that is potentially heretical yet can be celebrated orthodoxly?

5.3.1. Disobedience to Laws

First, we want to look at the principle of why one can be disobedient to laws at all. The introduction of changes to the liturgy is not a dogmatic decision concerning faith or morals, but a disciplinary question. With fallible disciplinary decisions, as with every law, the principle lex injusta non obligat applies. St. Thomas writes:[63]

“On the other hand laws may be unjust in two ways: first, by being contrary to human good, through being opposed to the things mentioned above - either in respect of the end, as when an authority imposes on his subjects burdensome laws, conducive, not to the common good, but rather to his own cupidity or vainglory - or in respect of the author, as when a man makes a law that goes beyond the power committed to him - or in respect of the form, as when burdens are imposed unequally on the community, although with a view to the common good. The like are acts of violence rather than laws; because, as Augustine says (De Lib. Arb. i, 5),”a law that is not just, seems to be no law at all.” Wherefore such laws do not bind in conscience, except perhaps in order to avoid scandal or disturbance, for which cause a man should even yield his right, according to Matthew 5:40-41: “If a man… take away thy coat, let go thy cloak also unto him; and whosoever will force thee one mile, go with him other two.””

An unjust law therefore does not bind, and for a law to be just it must serve the common good. Does the NO serve the common good? As we discussed above, the NO as a rite is bad in itself because it specifically gives room to obscure the essence of the Mass. That cannot serve the common good. Georg May writes:[64]

Original: “Nun wäre es unzutreffend, zu behaupten, der Ordo Missae Pauls VI. sei häretisch, wie es hie und da geschehen ist. Häresie ist die harte Leugnung von Wahrheiten, die mit göttlichem und katholischem Glauben anzunehmen sind (c. 1325 $ 2 CIC). Der neue Ordo Missae leugnet kein Dogma der Kirche. Das Schweigen von einer Lehre ist nicht gleichbedeutend mit ihrer Ablehnung. Indes ist oben nachgewiesen worden, daß der Ordo Missae Pauls VI. gegenüber dem Ordo Missae Pius’ V., dogmatisch gesehen, einen Rückschritt darstellt, daß er Glaube und Frömmigkeit des katholischen Volkes erheblich weniger fördert, ja gefährdet und daß er bedenkliche pastorale Schäden heraufführt. Das alles braucht hier nicht wiederholt zu werden. Vielmehr ist aus diesen Fakten die notwendige Folgerung zu ziehen. Diese kann nur lauten: Da feststeht, daß der Ordo Missae Pauls VI. den Anforderungen, die an die gleichbleibenden Teile der hl. Messe gestellt werden müssen, nicht entspricht; daß er Gefahren von ungeheurer Tragweite heraufbeschwört; daß er, kurz gesagt, dem Gemeinwohl der Kirche nicht dient, sondern ihm schadet, ergibt sich daraus, daß das ihn einführende Gesetz ein ungerechtes Gesetz ist. Es besitzt aus sich keine Verbindlichkeit, Die Priester und Gläubigen dürfen das Gesetz, das die Benutzung des Ordo Missa Pauls VI. vorschreibt, unbeachtet lassen. Sie dürfen ihn verwenden, falls die bei Nichtbenutzung eintretenden Schäden größer wären, also beispielsweise Priester bei Nichtbenutzung sicher ihre Stelle verlieren würden und dadurch ihre Gläubigen entweder verwaist oder einem progressistischen „Gemeindeleiter“ ausgeliefert sähen. Ihr Gewissen wird entscheiden müssen, was die Verantwortung für Glaube, Gottesdienst und Seelsorge gebietet.”

Translation: “Now it would be inaccurate to claim that the Ordo Missae of Paul VI is heretical, as has happened here and there. Heresy is the obstinate denial of truths that must be accepted with divine and Catholic faith (c. 1325 § 2 CIC). The new Ordo Missae denies no dogma of the Church. Silence about a doctrine is not the same as its rejection. Nevertheless, it has been demonstrated above that the Ordo Missae of Paul VI, compared with the Ordo Missae of Pius V, represents a regression from the dogmatic point of view; that it promotes the faith and piety of the Catholic people considerably less, indeed endangers them, and that it brings about serious pastoral harm. All this need not be repeated here. Rather, the necessary conclusion must be drawn from these facts. This conclusion can only be: Since it is established that the Ordo Missae of Paul VI does not correspond to the requirements that must be made of the unchanging parts of the Holy Mass; that it summons dangers of enormous scope; that, in short, it does not serve the common good of the Church but harms it, it follows that the law introducing it is an unjust law. It has no binding force of itself. Priests and faithful may disregard the law that prescribes the use of the Ordo Missae of Paul VI. They may use it if the harms arising from non-use would be greater, for example if priests would certainly lose their position by not using it and their faithful would thereby either be left orphaned or handed over to a progressive ‘parish leader.’ Their conscience will have to decide what responsibility for faith, worship, and pastoral care demands.”

5.3.2. Justification of the FSSPX

Since the introduction of the NO does not serve the common good and is therefore an unjust law, the FSSPX is free to refuse obedience to this law. This holds even if one adopts the moderate position. Nevertheless, beyond this, a justification is still needed insofar as even unjust laws bind to the extent required to avoid scandal or disorder. The justification here is that the celebration of and participation in the NO is, to a certain degree, an assent to the underlying heretical theology. Father Gaudron writes with respect to the best possible NO Masses:[52]

Original: “Bei der Beurteilung des NOM muß man das ganze Meßbuch vor Augen haben, und hier findet sich eben nicht nur eine konservative Form mit römischem Kanon, sondern ganz gleichberechtigt die Möglichkeit, die Messe so zu feiern, daß sie von einer protestantischen Abendmahlsfeier kaum unterscheidbar ist. Man kann ja auch nicht behaupten, die Bücher eines Hans Küng seien an sich gut, weil sich auf einigen Seiten auch richtige und schöne Gedanken neben den Häresien befinden. Wer die neue Messe annimmt, gibt damit seine grundsätzliche Zustimmung zur ganzen Liturgiereform, selbst wenn er den NOM persönlich nur in einer konservativen Form feiert.”

Translation: “In judging the NOM, one must keep the whole Missal in view, and here one finds not only a conservative form with the Roman Canon, but, on fully equal footing, the possibility of celebrating the Mass in such a way that it is hardly distinguishable from a Protestant Lord’s Supper service. After all, one cannot claim that the books of a Hans Küng are good in themselves because correct and beautiful thoughts are found on some pages alongside the heresies. Whoever accepts the New Mass thereby gives his fundamental assent to the entire liturgical reform, even if he personally celebrates the NOM only in a conservative form.”

The application to the individual will certainly differ from person to person. As described in the preceding quotation from Georg May, in the current Church crisis it is the right decision for some pastors to celebrate the NO. In my assessment, it is also generally better for the faithful to attend the NO on Sundays than no Mass at all. But this too depends on the circumstances. I basically agree with the FSSPX that participation in the NO should be passive, precisely in order to avoid giving full outward assent or acceptance to the liturgical reform and thereby causing scandal. However, this attitude can also be expressed in individual cases even when one participates actively, for example if one is the only person who receives Communion on the tongue and deliberately goes not to an extraordinary minister of Communion but to the pastor. For the FSSPX, however, the situation is different. Both at the time of its introduction and today, celebration of the NO by its priests would clearly signal outwardly that the dogmatic objections raised are no longer maintained. It would be a clearly scandalous assent to the underlying false image of the essence of the Mass, and therefore it must be avoided uncompromisingly. I want to explain this more concretely in what follows.

5.3.3. The Concept of Scandal in Thomas and the FSSPX

Thomas defines scandal in ST II-II q.43 a.1 as:

“dictum vel factum minus rectum praebens alteri occasionem ruinae”: a word or action less rightly done that gives another occasion for ruin.

The decisive word here is dictum - word, statement. Thomas means by this that an action can have a communicative character. It says something, even if it explicitly asserts nothing. The scandal then lies not only in the action itself, but in what it means to the observer. Thomas further distinguishes between active scandal (scandalum datum) and passive scandal (scandalum acceptum). For our question, only active scandal is relevant: the giver of the action is responsible for the confusion he causes. Now we want to show that celebration of the NO by FSSPX priests would be active scandal in the Thomistic sense. The argument has three steps:

5.3.3.1. First Step: The Action Has a Communicative Character

For decades the FSSPX has maintained a clearly articulated dogmatic position on the NO. This position is public, institutional, and inseparably connected with the name of the FSSPX. As a result, every action of an FSSPX priest with respect to the NO necessarily has semantic content: it is not a neutral liturgical act, but an implicit statement about the validity of the objections raised. If an FSSPX priest celebrates the NO, he does not merely say: “I consider this rite suboptimal, but I celebrate it anyway.” Whether he wants to or not, he says: “The dogmatic objections for which my community stands no longer hold.” This is a dictum in the Thomistic sense - a statement through action.

5.3.3.2. Second Step: This Statement Gives Occasion for Spiritual Ruin

First it must be emphasized that the scandal is not limited to the circle of FSSPX supporters. Because the action has a communicative character, its statement is directed to everyone who perceives it and knows the position of the FSSPX. What it communicates is an objectively false proposition: that a publicly advanced, dogmatically grounded criticism that has never been substantively refuted may be abandoned under institutional pressure. This is scandalum datum in the strict sense, active scandal, because the action itself says something wrong, regardless of who receives it. It suggests that truth yields to authority, and this is precisely the basic Modernist movement against which the entire resistance is directed. The scandal here is therefore not fed by a self-created expectation, but by the objective situation: a true and unanswered criticism that would be revoked. Beyond this, especially grave harm is done to that group which has entrusted itself to the FSSPX precisely because of the dogmatic seriousness of its position. On the basis of FSSPX teaching, these faithful have developed a certain understanding of the Mass, an awareness of the sacrificial character and of the Protestantizing tendencies of the NO rite. If the FSSPX priest now celebrates the NO, they will either

  1. conclude that the objections were not so serious after all and abandon their dogmatic vigilance, which is a real spiritual harm, or
  2. fall into deep confusion about the essence of the Mass, likewise a spiritual harm.

Both correspond exactly to what Thomas calls ruina spiritualis: not necessarily a formal sin, but a real regression in faith.

5.3.3.3. Third Step: It Is scandalum pusillorum

In q.43 a.7, Thomas distinguishes between the scandal of the weak (pusilli) and the scandal of the Pharisees. The scandal of the Pharisees - that is, scandal arising from malice or narrow-mindedness - need not be avoided if doing so would come at the expense of truth. The scandal of the weak, however, must be avoided even when the action itself would be permitted. Thomas writes:

Original: “Sicut Augustinus dicit […] debemus temporalia relinquere propter scandalum vitandum”

Translation: “As Augustine says, […] we must give up temporal goods in order to avoid scandal.”

The faithful of the FSSPX and all observers are here the pusilli in the theological sense - not because they are stupid, but because in a Church crisis they depend on the leadership of the FSSPX to preserve their faith in the sacrifice of the Mass. An FSSPX that celebrates the NO takes this leadership away from them.

5.3.3.4. A Self-Made Problem?

One might perhaps object that this is therefore a home-made problem. If the FSSPX had not resisted, it would not have to resist. But this ignores the fact that the resistance was necessary from the beginning, as shown, among other things, by the fact that the heretical formula in IGMR 7 was softened.

5.4. Conclusion

Let us summarize the argument. The starting point was Father Gaudron’s formal argument: because a rite essentially has the task of making clear the essence of the Mass, and because the NO fails precisely in this task, it is bad in itself. The first two premises, the Thomistic definition of evil as privation and the determination of the Mass supported by Trent and Mediator Dei, are hardly disputable. Only the third premise is contested. In the moderate position, I proposed weakening this third premise: it is enough to show that the NO leaves too much room to obscure the sacrificial character. This formulation is supported by the Protestantizing tendencies, the original IGMR definition, the arbitrariness regarding the Suscipiat, ad populum celebration, Communion in the hand, and extraordinary ministers of Communion, and it nevertheless leads to the same result: the NO as a rite is bad in itself, even if an individual Mass celebrated according to it can be good. But that does not yet decide the matter, because a rite bad in itself could nevertheless be legitimately prescribed by the Church. Here, however, the principle lex injusta non obligat applies: a law that prescribes a rite that does not serve the common good of the Church is not binding. Georg May showed this in detail. This argument is independent of the FSSPX; it applies to every priest and every member of the faithful who accepts the above premises. The question whether one may resist is therefore objectively answered. What remains is only the question how far this resistance must go. In principle, a broad spectrum can be legitimate here: from the priest who, according to Georg May’s standard, continues to celebrate the NO in order not to abandon his faithful to progressive parish leaders, all the way to complete refusal. For the FSSPX, however, the situation is no longer an open weighing of options. The dogmatic objections that support its resistance were substantively justified from the beginning, as even the later softening of the heretical IGMR definition shows. As long as they have not been answered, every celebration of the NO by FSSPX priests would not mean “reconciliation,” but the revocation of a criticism that has never been refuted, and thus would spiritually harm precisely those faithful who have relied on the seriousness of this criticism (scandalum pusillorum in the sense of ST II-II q.43). The refusal to celebrate the NO is therefore not disobedience out of narrow-mindedness. It follows from two steps valid for all Catholics: the rite fails to fulfill an essential task, and the law prescribing it therefore does not bind; and from a third step that arises only in the concrete situation of the FSSPX, but precisely there because the underlying criticism is substantively correct and not because it was once loudly voiced.

6. The Announced Consecrations

Finally, we now want to examine whether the illicit episcopal consecrations are also justified. Just because the refusal to sign V2 is justified and the refusal to celebrate the NO is justified, it does not yet follow that the illicit episcopal consecration is justified too. May one violate the prohibition? Is the requirement of the pope’s consent divine law? Are such consecrations necessarily schismatic? Do they automatically lead to excommunication? These are the questions I want to answer here.

6.1. Illicit Episcopal Consecrations in Canon Law

Canon 1013 says:[65]

Original: “Keinem Bischof ist es gestattet, jemanden zum Bischof zu weihen, wenn nicht zuvor der päpstliche Auftrag feststeht.”

Translation: “No bishop is permitted to consecrate anyone a bishop unless it is first established that there is a pontifical mandate.”

What, then, justifies violating this canon law? We can approach this question from two distinct but nevertheless related legal principles: the principle of Epikie and the maxim necessitas legem non habet.

6.1.1. Epikie

Epikie (from Greek ἐπιείκεια, forbearance, leniency), one of the oldest virtues, according to St. Thomas:[66]

Original: “die habituelle Bereitschaft, die inneren u. äußeren, oft außergewöhnl. Umstände genau abzuwägen u. eine Handlung im Lichte der den Einzelnormen übergeordneten eth. Prinzipien zu beurteilen”.

Translation: “the habitual readiness to weigh carefully the inner and outer, often extraordinary circumstances and to judge an action in the light of the ethical principles superior to the individual norms.”

In short, Epikie is the principle of disregarding the letter of a law in individual cases when obeying the law in that individual case would contradict the purpose of the law. St. Thomas permits the application of this principle in cases:[67]

Original: “in denen die wörtliche Befolgung des Gesetzes verwerflich wäre, (…) möglicherweise auch in anderen Fällen, in denen der Gesetzgeber nicht bereit wäre, den Untertanen zu binden, wenn er selbst anwesend wäre.”

Translation: “in which literal observance of the law would be blameworthy, (…) possibly also in other cases in which the legislator would not be willing to bind the subject if he himself were present.”

For St. Thomas, the justification for Epikie results from the fact that the legislator cannot foresee all individual circumstances and therefore must issue laws that cover the supermajority of cases.[68] This makes clear that when St. Thomas speaks of the “legislator (…) being present,” he means the original legislator, and not necessarily the current holder of legislative authority. The latter could reinterpret and abuse the intention of the law. His example is the following:[69]

Original: “Das Gesetz besagt, dass ein hinterlegter Gegenstand zurückgegeben werden muss, wenn der Eigentümer ihn verlangt. Befindet sich der Besitzer einer Waffe jedoch in einem Wutanfall, wenn er sie zurückfordert, und besteht die ernsthafte Gefahr, dass er jemanden verletzen könnte, ist es nicht richtig, sich an die Norm zu halten. In diesem Fall ist es angebracht, das Gesetz zu ignorieren und dem sogenannten „Gerechtigkeitssinn“ zu folgen.”

Translation: “The law states that a deposited object must be returned when its owner asks for it. If, however, the owner of a weapon is in a fit of rage when he asks for it back, and there is serious danger that he could injure someone, it is not right to adhere to the norm. In this case it is appropriate to ignore the law and to follow the so-called ‘sense of justice.’”

Epikie applies to all human positive law, both civil and ecclesiastical.[70] How can this principle now be applied to the case of the FSSPX? The prohibition of illicit episcopal consecrations as it stands today in canon law was formulated under John Paul II. Canon 1387 says:[71]

Original: “Ein Bischof, der jemanden ohne päpstlichen Auftrag zum Bischof weiht, und ebenso, wer von ihm die Weihe empfängt, ziehen sich die dem Apostolischen Stuhl vorbehaltene Exkommunikation als Tatstrafe zu.”

Translation: “A bishop who consecrates someone a bishop without a pontifical mandate, and likewise the person who receives the consecration from him, incurs a latae sententiae excommunication reserved to the Apostolic See.”

But this is not the origin of the law. Its origin lies in the Investiture Controversy, in which Pope Gregory VII in Dictatus Papae first claimed the exclusive right to appoint bishops. He wrote literally:[72]

Original: “Dass er allein Bischöfe absetzen und wieder einsetzen kann.”

Translation: “That he alone can depose and reinstate bishops.”

Several hundred years later, it was first written into canon law by Pope Benedict XV. There Canon 953 still said:

“The consecration of a Bishop is reserved to the Roman Pontiff so that it is not permitted to any Bishop to consecrate another as Bishop without first having gotten a pontifical mandate.”

Canon 2370 then says:[73]

“A Bishop consecrating another Bishop, and the assistant Bishops or, in the place of Bishops, priests, and those who receive consecration without an apostolic mandate against the prescription of Canon 953 are by the law suspended until the Apostolic See dispenses them”

This prohibition is then condemned once more in the magisterium under Pius XII, who writes in Ad apostolorum principis (AAP) 39:[74]

“Granted this exception, it follows that bishops who have been neither named nor confirmed by the Apostolic See, but who, on the contrary, have been elected and consecrated in defiance of its express orders, enjoy no powers of teaching or of jurisdiction since jurisdiction passes to bishops only through the Roman Pontiff as We admonished in the Encyclical Letter Mystici Corporis in the following words:”… As far as his own diocese is concerned each (bishop) feeds the flock entrusted to him as a true shepherd and rules it in the name of Christ. Yet in exercising this office they are not altogether independent but are subordinate to the lawful authority of the Roman Pontiff, although enjoying ordinary power of jurisdiction which they receive directly from the same Supreme Pontiff.””

In order to apply the principle of Epikie to the consecrations of the FSSPX, we must first ask who the legislator is. As we have seen above, this is not about the current holder of legislative power but about the original legislator. Since the canon under John Paul II only changed the penalty for illicit episcopal consecration, but not the prohibition as such, we can regard Benedict XV as the original legislator. For even though the new canon law replaces the old and draws its legitimacy fundamentally from the authority of the pope, it nevertheless refers to the 1917 Code of Canon Law when it says in Canon 6:

Original: “Die Canones dieses Codex sind, soweit sie altes Recht wiedergeben, auch unter Berücksichtigung der kanonischen Tradition zu würdigen.”

Translation: “The canons of this Code, insofar as they reproduce old law, are also to be assessed with due regard for canonical tradition.”

The new canon law is therefore also to be seen as a continuation of the old canon law, to be read in its tradition, though of course where there are contradictions it is not bound by the old law. Insofar as it is to be understood in this way, Benedict XV can therefore be regarded as the legislator of the prohibition, even though John Paul II adjusted the associated penalty. One can now ask whether Benedict XV, had he been present in the case of the FSSPX episcopal consecrations, would have approved the non-application of his law, since it represents an unforeseen consequence of his law in an individual case. Here it must be mentioned that the codification of the prohibition, as previously noted, goes back to condemnations by earlier popes who acted in the context of state interference in Church affairs. It was not developed in the context of internal Church conflicts and was only later applied to them. Benedict XV condemned Modernism in his encyclical Ad Beatissimi Apostolorum (ABA) 25 and wrote:[75]

“Hence arose the monstrous errors of”Modernism,” which Our Predecessor rightly declared to be “the synthesis of all heresies,” and solemnly condemned. We hereby renew that condemnation in all its fulness, Venerable Brethren, and as the plague is not yet entirely stamped out, but lurks here and there in hidden places, We exhort all to be carefully here and there in hidden places, We exhort all to be carefully on their guard against any contagion of the evil, to which we may apply the words Job used in other circumstances: “It is a fire that devoureth even to destruction, and rooteth up all things that spring” (Job xxxi. 12). Nor do We merely desire that Catholics should shrink from the errors of Modernism, but also from the tendencies or what is called the spirit of Modernism. Those who are infected by that spirit develop a keen dislike for all that savours of antiquity and become eager searchers after novelties in everything: in the way in which they carry out religious functions, in the ruling of Catholic institutions, and even in private exercises of piety. Therefore it is Our will that the law of our forefathers should still be held sacred: “Let there be no innovation; keep to what has been handed down.””

Thus, if one considers that Benedict XV strongly condemned the Modernism that entered through V2, and if one considers that his prohibition of illicit episcopal consecrations arises from the Investiture Controversy and not from internal Church conflicts, we have a good basis for assuming that he would approve the non-application of his law to this individual case, because at the time he enacted the law he could not foresee such a case. We can summarize the argument from Epikie as follows: The prohibition of illicit episcopal consecrations has an identifiable legislative history with an identifiable purpose: to protect the Church from the usurpation of the episcopal office by external powers. From Gregory VII through Trent to Benedict XV, the context is consistent: Investiture Controversy, state-church systems, Josephinism, communist interference. The legislator wanted to prevent secular power from deciding over the occupation of episcopal sees. The FSSPX situation is structurally inverse: here a bishop consecrates without a pontifical mandate not to serve an external power, but to preserve the received order of the Church against a perceived internal deviation. This constellation - an internal Church conflict over Tradition in which the governing authority itself appears as the problem - is simply absent from the legislative tradition of the prohibition. The decisive Thomistic point is then this: the legislator Benedict XV cannot have willed, in concreto, that his prohibition should apply precisely when its actual protective purpose - the preservation of the episcopal office in its received form - is at stake. To apply the law literally here produces an outcome that contradicts the spirit of the law. And that is the classic case of Epikie. The fact that John Paul II explicitly refused the mandate does not necessarily weaken the argument, because for Epikie what is decisive is not the current expression of will of the lawgiver, but the original intention of the law. If the current holder of legislative power applies the law in a way that contradicts its own history of origin, then this is precisely the case in which Epikie applies - and not the case in which it is excluded.

6.1.2. Necessitas legem non habet

The second legal principle under which we can place the consecrations of the FSSPX is the maxim Necessitas legem non habet, that is, “necessity has no law.” Unlike Epikie, this is not about interpreting a law against its letter in the sense of the legislator’s intention, that is, not about an operation within the legal order, but about the priority of a higher norm over positive law. Epikie applies the law, only not according to its letter. Necessitas suspends the binding force of the law because a good standing above it is at stake. Thomas treats the principle using the classic example of theft from hunger in ST II-II q.66 a.7. There he asks whether someone in a state of necessity may take another’s property, and answers in the affirmative:[76]

“Nevertheless, if the need be so manifest and urgent, that it is evident that the present need must be remedied by whatever means be at hand (for instance when a person is in some imminent danger, and there is no other possible remedy), then it is lawful for a man to succor his own need by means of another’s property, by taking it either openly or secretly: nor is this properly speaking theft or robbery.”

This is the clean Necessitas structure. The right of property remains intact as a norm and is not reinterpreted. What happens is that, in this concrete situation, it yields to a higher norm: the protection of life, which follows from natural law itself. Thomas formulates the general principle at the end of ST I-II q.96 a.6 in the classic maxim necessitas non habet legem.[77] Necessitas is therefore not a component of positive law that the respective legal community could establish or abolish at will, but a priority rule standing above positive law, following from natural law and from the essence of human legislation. It is meta-juridical in the strict sense: it operates above positive law and determines when positive law must yield. In canon law, this priority finds its most prominent expression in Canon 1752, the final canon of the 1983 CIC: salus animarum, quae in Ecclesia suprema semper lex esse debet, the salvation of souls must always be the supreme law in the Church.[78] This canon determines what applies in conflicts between positive Church laws and higher goods. If a positive law does not promote the salvation of souls but endangers it, then it must yield. That is exactly the canonical form of the Necessitas principle. Let us apply this to the episcopal consecrations of the FSSPX. The prohibition of consecration without a pontifical mandate is positive Church law. It is not divine law, as we will show in the next section, and therefore falls under the Necessitas rule. The question is therefore whether in 1988 there was a state of necessity that endangered the good of “the salvation of souls” so gravely that the positive prohibition had to yield. The necessity relevant here is not the preservation of a rite or of a liturgical style, but the Modernist crisis itself discussed in the preceding sections. If Pius X is right that Modernism is the “synthesis of all heresies,” and if the indicators collected in the preceding sections are correct - the voting behavior of the German bishops, the state of belief among clergy according to studies, the ambiguities embedded in V2 in DH, LG XVI, and LG VIII and their reception, the structural problems of the NO, the absence of canonical reactions against bishops and theologians teaching Modernistically - then this is a situation in which the salvation of souls is endangered systematically and over generations. This is necessity in the technical sense, because the one good to which the Church is ultimately obliged is placed at stake by the failure of her own disciplinary and doctrinal structures. This brings us to the decisive counter-question. In EDA of July 2, 1988, John Paul II expressly denied the existence of a state of necessity:[79]

“In particular, one cannot affirm a ‘state of necessity’, by which anyone would be permitted to confer episcopal ordination contrary to the will of the Supreme Pontiff, Supreme Pastor of the Universal Church.”

Is the discussion thereby ended? Not if one takes seriously the specific nature of this judgment. The statement “there is no state of necessity” is not a magisterial judgment about faith or morals, but a factual determination about the concrete situation of the Church. It therefore does not fall under the conditions of papal infallibility as the First Vatican Council explicitly outlined them in Pastor Aeternus, chapter 4 (DS 3074):

Original: “Der Römische Bischof besitzt, wenn er ex cathedra spricht, das heißt, wenn er, das Amt des Hirten und Lehrers aller Christen ausübend, kraft seiner höchsten apostolischen Autorität eine Lehre über Glauben oder Sitten als von der ganzen Kirche festzuhaltende definiert, durch den göttlichen Beistand, der ihm im seligen Petrus verheißen ist, jene Unfehlbarkeit, mit der der göttliche Erlöser seine Kirche bei der Definition einer Lehre über Glauben oder Sitten ausgestattet wissen wollte.”

Translation: “The Roman Pontiff possesses, when he speaks ex cathedra, that is, when, exercising the office of shepherd and teacher of all Christians, by virtue of his supreme apostolic authority, he defines a doctrine concerning faith or morals to be held by the whole Church, through the divine assistance promised to him in blessed Peter, that infallibility with which the divine Redeemer willed His Church to be endowed in defining doctrine concerning faith or morals.”

Three conditions are named here: an ex cathedra act, doctrine concerning faith or morals, and proposal for acceptance by the whole Church. EDA is a motu proprio with disciplinary and fact-finding character and fulfills none of them. Ott confirms this a contrario in his Fundamentals of Catholic Dogma when he states:[80]

“The ordinary and usual form of the Papal teaching activity is not infallible. Further, the decisions of the Roman Congregations (Holy Office, Bible Commission) are not infallible.”

If even the ordinary papal magisterium in the narrower sense is not infallible, then still less is a determination about the ecclesial situation embedded in a disciplinary act. A critic might object here that papal infallibility must be extended beyond faith and morals to disciplinary factual judgments of this kind, because otherwise precisely that private jurisdiction arises against which we want to guard. But this extension itself is not supported by the Magisterium. Whoever wanted to extend infallibility to such judgments would burst the limits formulated in Pastor Aeternus and thereby introduce a new doctrine for which there is no support in the Magisterium. That papal factual and disciplinary judgments could very well err in Church history is shown by two precedents already partly addressed in the preceding sections. The first is the Arian crisis. Athanasius resisted not only an overwhelming majority of bishops and a pope yielding under pressure; he effectively resisted the factual assessment prevailing in the hierarchy at that time, and he had no basis for this other than his own judgment about the theological and ecclesial situation. Had he asked Liberius in exile at Beroea whether there was a state of necessity justifying his resistance, he would hardly have received an affirmative answer. Athanasius was later canonized. The second and structurally even more fitting precedent is Eusebius of Dorylaeum. Around Christmas 428, shortly after Patriarch Nestorius had rejected the title Theotokos for the Virgin Mary in a sermon, Eusebius, then still a layman, lawyer, and agens in rebus at the imperial court in Constantinople, stood up in the middle of the liturgy and publicly contradicted him with the confession that the eternal Word, begotten before all ages, had also submitted Himself to the second birth according to the flesh (cf. Cyril of Alexandria, Adv. Nestor. I, 20, PG 76, 41D; Marius Mercator, Liber subnotationum, PL 48, 769B). Shortly thereafter he circulated in the city the contestatio pamphlet (CPG 5940/8620), in which he compared Nestorius with Paul of Samosata.[81] What is decisive for our argument is that Eusebius took this step without consulting a higher authority. At that time he had neither episcopal nor teaching authority; he was a layman and based his resistance solely on his own judgment about the theological situation against the reigning patriarch of one of the most important episcopal sees of Christendom. Nor did he have the excuse that no higher authority was reachable. Pope Celestine I resided in Rome and was orthodox. Eusebius could theoretically have turned to him and asked for an assessment of the situation. He did not do so. The formal accusation against Nestorius before Celestine was later pursued not by Eusebius but chiefly by Cyril of Alexandria (cf. Cyril’s Epistula ad Coelestinum, ACO I,1,5). Eusebius himself acted before and independently of this higher involvement. Strictly speaking, Eusebius did not violate positive law, but publicly contradicted his bishop. His case is therefore not a direct Necessitas versus law case in the narrow sense. What it does show, however, is precisely the contested point of our argument: that the judgment “there is an ecclesial state of necessity” is not the privilege of the highest authority and does not presuppose its prior confirmation. Eusebius made such a factual determination independently as a layman, and the Church confirmed it retroactively and even explicitly. This confirmation is not only structural, through the later condemnation of Nestorius, but also direct in the papal word. On August 11, 430, in his Epistola XIV ad clerum et populum Constantinopolitanum, that is, before the Council of Ephesus, Celestine writes expressly to the Constantinopolitan laity and clergy who resisted Nestorius, and calls their situation blessed precisely when they themselves must judge their pasture because the shepherd has become a wolf:[82]

Original: “Quid spei habeat grex, quando lupum se ipse pastor ostendit, et sic oves invadit, ut grassetur in singulas? (…) Beatus tamen grex cui dedit Dominus de pascuis judicare.” (PL 50, 505)

Translation: “What hope has the flock when the shepherd himself shows himself to be a wolf and invades the sheep so as to rage against each one? (…) Yet blessed is the flock to whom the Lord has given to judge concerning the pastures.” (PL 50, 505)

But Celestine goes further. He explicitly places Athanasius before the resisters as an example:

Original: “Habetis vos quicumque ecclesia estis ejecti, pene nostrorum temporum beatae recordationis Athanasii Alexandrinae Ecclesiae prudentissimi sacerdotis exemplum. Cui non consolationi sit illius tolerantia? cui non sit exemplo illius fortitudo?” (PL 50, 509)

Translation: “You, whoever have been cast out of the Church, have almost in our own times the example of Athanasius of blessed memory, the most prudent priest of the Church of Alexandria. To whom is his endurance not a consolation? To whom is his courage not an example?” (PL 50, 509)

And finally he declares the canonical sanctions with which Nestorius had punished the resisters - removals from office, excommunications - to be retroactively null:

Original: “aperte sedis nostrae sanxit auctoritas, nullum sive episcopum, sive clericum, seu professione aliqua Christianum, qui a Nestorio vel ejus similibus, ex quo talia praedicare coeperunt, vel loco suo vel communione dejecti sunt, vel ejectum vel excommunicatum videri.” (PL 50, 510)

Translation: “the authority of our See has openly decreed that no bishop, cleric, or Christian of any profession who, by Nestorius or by those like him, since they began to preach such things, has been cast down from his place or communion, is to be regarded as deposed or excommunicated.” (PL 50, 510)

This is not only structural but explicit papal confirmation. The judgment of the laity and clergy in Constantinople about the situation of their Church, made without prior consultation with Rome, was correct. The canonical sanctions imposed on them by the competent hierarchy for it were null. Eusebius himself later became bishop of Dorylaeum and was honored at the Council of Chalcedon in 451 as a defender of orthodoxy, in whose acts he brings the accusation against Eutyches (ACO II,1,1). Both precedents, Athanasius and Eusebius, therefore show structurally the same thing: the judgment about the existence of an ecclesial state of necessity is not reserved exclusively to the highest authority, at least not when that authority itself is compromised by political pressure (Liberius under the Arian emperor) or by theological decay (Nestorius as patriarch, and in Father Schmidberger’s diagnosis the Modernist basic attitudes of today’s popes). In both cases, judgment was made against the institutional tendency and without prior confirmation from above, and in both cases the Church later explicitly confirmed the judgments. That resistance could in fact be offered independently of Rome is, in the case of Athanasius, explainable because of the possibility of consulting Liberius only by the compromised state of that authority; in the case of Eusebius it is even clearer, because an orthodox higher authority existed in Rome to whom he could have turned, yet he still saw himself compelled to an independent judgment. This brings us to the last and weightiest objection, that of private jurisdiction. The critic says: if I can decide for myself whether to obey law X, why not every law? Father Schmidberger admitted in a personal conversation that the Necessitas argument structurally does indeed open this door. But the answer does not lie in closing this door in principle, for this is precisely the whole point of the doctrine of Necessitas; rather, it lies in the theological and epistemological justification for why one must pass through it here and how one prevents this from collapsing into arbitrariness. First, Thomas teaches that conscience binds absolutely, even an erroneous conscience, because human judgment is fundamentally required in every moral question (cf. ST I-II q.19 a.5). This does not lead to relativism, because conscience must be ordered to the objective moral law; it is only an expression of our epistemic finitude. Christ did not grant His Church infallibility in every act, but only under the precisely defined conditions listed by Pastor Aeternus. Outside these conditions we must judge according to our best conscience and can err in doing so. The same applies to every Necessitas judgment. It can be mistaken, but it can also be correct, and the possibility of error does not abolish the principle. The later ecclesial confirmation did not make the resistance of Athanasius and Eusebius correct retroactively; it merely showed it to have been correct. The act was already right or wrong at the moment of action, independently of whether and when a judgment from above followed. Two things follow from this. First, the absence of such confirmation in the case of the FSSPX says nothing about the rightness of its action, but only something about our present certainty regarding it. Second, no objection can be built from this that makes prior confirmation a condition of permitted action. Whoever demands that falls into an infinite regress: if the individual may never judge for himself whether a situation exists, then one authority would have to determine that it exists, another that this determination is correct, and so on. Even the act by which a believer recognizes which authority is the legitimate Magisterium at all is such an act of personal judgment (the classical praeambula fidei). The possibility of independent judgment therefore cannot be denied without falling into epistemic nihilism. The question is never whether the individual judges, but only how high the threshold lies at which his judgment outweighs the presumption in favor of authority, and precisely this threshold is set high by the conditions named above: qualified competence, gravity of the situation, proportionality. Second: the salvation of souls stands above all human laws, and we have no guarantee from Christ that positive Church law can never contradict this higher law. Pastor Aeternus promised infallibility to the Church only in a clearly circumscribed domain, and disciplinary individual decisions and factual determinations about the situation of the Church do not belong to this domain. Whoever claims that the pope cannot err in the question whether a state of necessity exists extends infallibility beyond what the Magisterium itself claims. Incidentally, this is precisely the error against which Donum Veritatis warns under the heading of avoiding maximalism. Third, and this is the decisive empirical point: if the Necessitas argument really tipped over into private jurisdiction, this would show itself in the practice of the FSSPX. It does not show itself. The FSSPX draws clear boundaries that it does not overstep. It does not celebrate the still older Holy Week rite from the time before Pius XII, although it prefers it liturgical-historically, because it acknowledges that this reform alone does not yet establish a state of necessity justifying such resistance. It claims no territorial jurisdiction, asserts no power of suspension over other clergy, and otherwise keeps the list of conciliar texts it rejects deliberately narrow. Whoever had truly tipped over into private jurisdiction would not maintain such self-limitation. The question of proportionality remains. A state of necessity justifies only the minimal intervention needed to deal with it. Were the episcopal consecrations in 1988 this minimal intervention? Here the canonically recognized alternatives must be examined. The Ecclesia Dei communities such as the FSSP and ICKSP can preserve the traditional liturgy, but they are bound to full acceptance of Vatican II and cannot publicly criticize the problematic texts from DH, LG XVI, and LG VIII as contradicting Tradition. But whoever wants to address the Modernist crisis at the root must do exactly that. Silent resistance within the existing structures fails even more fundamentally because it cannot be extended to priestly formation. Without independent traditional seminaries, and without bishops who supervise these seminaries and ordain new priests, Tradition breaks off over two generations. The episcopal consecrations of 1988 were therefore not an addition to the resistance, but its actual foundation, because they made it reproducible beyond Lefebvre’s death. If one takes the state of necessity seriously as a diagnosis, then the consecrations were the proportionally minimal answer, not maximalism.

6.1.3. Conclusion on Canon Law

We have therefore seen that there are two established meta-juridical principles that can be applied to the FSSPX episcopal consecrations and justify not submitting to canon law in this case. Whether Epikie is actually applicable in this case is certainly disputable. One can argue about whether Benedict XV is in fact the relevant legislator and whether he would have permitted the FSSPX episcopal consecrations as an individual exception. But because a good argument can be made for this, it should at least be mentioned here, even if this question probably cannot be settled definitively. The more relevant argument is Necessitas, because this is the justification on which the FSSPX itself relies. For that reason I defended this argument more extensively, and we have reached the conclusion that it is in fact successful.

6.2. Is the Prohibition Not Divine Law?

Epikie and Necessitas both apply only if the law in question is not divine law. But this is precisely what some critics claim. In my opinion, this understanding rests on a false post-conciliar view that episcopal consecration necessarily also brings jurisdiction with it. That this is not the case, that permission for episcopal consecrations is therefore not divine law, and that Epikie and Necessitas therefore apply, is what I want to defend in what follows.

6.2.1. Why Believe It Is Divine Law?

Why should anyone think at all that it is divine law that the pope must permit episcopal consecrations? The statement is not found in the Bible; if anything, it is not directly revealed divine law. If it is divine law, then it would have to be implicit in revelation. Take papal infallibility as an example: a very new dogma, never clearly proclaimed in revelation. That it is nevertheless implicit in revelation is found by asking whether denying a statement leads to denying an already clearly formulated doctrine. In the case of the pope’s infallibility, one will find that if one denies this doctrine, one must either deny that the Church can be infallible or deny that the pope is the head of the Church. If one is logically consistent, then denying papal infallibility leads to denying less disputed doctrines. The situation is analogous with the claim that the necessity of papal permission for the consecration of bishops (NPEWB) is divine law. Here the argument is that whoever rejects NPEWB also rejects the pope’s universal jurisdiction. This reasoning is also found in the best-known proof text for NPEWB, the encyclical Ad apostolorum principis (AAP), where Pius XII quotes his letter Ad Sinarum gentem (ASG) as follows:[83]

“The power of jurisdiction which is conferred directly by divine right on the Supreme Pontiff comes to bishops by that same right, but only through the successor of Peter, to whom not only the faithful but also all bishops are bound to be constantly subject and to adhere both by the reverence of obedience and by the bond of unity.”

NPEWB as divine law therefore rests on the universal jurisdictional power of the pope, which was formulated most clearly in Pastor aeternus (PA), where chapter 3 says:[84]

Original: “Wenn also jemand sagt, der römische Papst habe nur das Amt der Aufsicht oder der Leitung, nicht aber die volle und höchste Jurisdiktionsgewalt über die gesamte Kirche, nicht bloß in Sachen des Glaubens und der Sitten, sondern auch in Sachen, welche die Disziplin und Regierung der über den ganzen Erdkreis verbreiteten Kirche betreffen; oder derselbe habe nur den vorzüglicheren Anteil, nicht aber die ganze Fülle dieser höchsten Gewalt; oder diese seine Gewalt sei nicht eine ordentliche und unmittelbare, sei es über alle und die einzelnen Kirchen oder über und die einzelnen Hirten und Gläubigen, so sei er im Banne.”

Translation: “Therefore, if anyone says that the Roman Pontiff has only the office of inspection or direction, but not full and supreme jurisdictional power over the whole Church, not only in matters of faith and morals, but also in those matters which concern the discipline and government of the Church spread throughout the whole world; or that he has only the more important part, but not the whole fullness of this supreme power; or that this power of his is not ordinary and immediate, whether over all and each of the Churches or over all and each of the pastors and faithful, let him be anathema.”

The argument is therefore that whoever rejects NPEWB as divine law also rejects the pope’s universal jurisdictional power, which PA explicitly condemns.

6.2.2. Episcopal Consecration and Jurisdictional Power

The question now is whether the consecration of bishops necessarily arrogates jurisdiction. If this is not the case, then it follows that NPEWB is divine law only when jurisdiction is also conferred, but otherwise only ecclesiastical positive law. LG 21 does indeed teach:[85]

Original: “Die Bischofsweihe überträgt mit dem Amt der Heiligung auch die Ämter der Lehre und der Leitung”

Translation: “Episcopal consecration confers, together with the office of sanctifying, also the offices of teaching and governing.”

The problem with this, however, is that it is itself an innovation of V2 that contradicts Tradition. Pius XII teaches the exact opposite in ASG 12 when he writes:[86]

“By virtue of God’s Will, the faithful are divided into two classes: the clergy and the laity. By virtue of the same Will is established the twofold sacred hierarchy, namely, of orders and jurisdiction. Besides - as has also been divinely established - the power of orders (through which the ecclesiastical hierarchy is composed of Bishops, priests, and ministers) comes from receiving the Sacrament of Holy Orders. But the power of jurisdiction, which is conferred upon the Supreme Pontiff directly by divine rights, flows to the Bishops by the same right, but only through the Successor of St. Peter, to whom not only the simple faithful, but even all the Bishops must be constantly subject, and to whom they must be bound by obedience and with the bond of unity.”

The statement from LG is therefore only another example of a clear contradiction with Tradition. That LG is false is also shown by classic examples of bishops without jurisdiction:

  1. Auxiliary bishops: they help the diocesan bishop with ceremonial tasks such as ordinations and confirmations, but possess no proper authority over the faithful of the diocese.
  2. Titular bishops: they symbolically receive the title of a defunct diocese, for example in North Africa, but exercise no authority there.
  3. Bishops emeriti: in modern law, bishops resign their jurisdiction at the age of 75, but remain bishops by their consecration.

Ratzinger himself admits this innovation when he says:

” Indeed, the rigid dividing line that had stood for centuries in the minds of most Western theologians between the power of order and the power of jurisdiction is now becoming surmountable,”

(La collégialité: développement théologique in L’Église de Vatican II, vol. 3, Cerf, collection Unam sanctam, 51c, p. 751, quoted in [87]) This new understanding is also reflected in the new canon law, where the prohibition moved from the section “Offenses against the administration of the sacraments” in the old canon law to the section “Offenses against the unity of the Church” in the new canon law.

6.2.3. Conclusion

We can therefore see that NPEWB is not per se divine law. It is divine law only insofar as it denies the full and universal jurisdictional power of the pope, but this is not per se the case with an episcopal consecration; it is the case only when the episcopal office comes with jurisdictional power. In the case of the FSSPX, however, these are mere auxiliary bishops who arrogate no jurisdictional power to themselves. Thus their consecration is not a violation of divine law, but only of ecclesiastical positive law, and it can be right under the justificatory grounds listed above.

6.3. Are Illicit Episcopal Consecrations Not Automatically Schismatic?

The claim that illicit episcopal consecrations are schismatic rests partly on the false understanding refuted above, namely that jurisdictional power necessarily accompanies consecration. But the claim also rests partly on the belief that schism is constituted by wanting to withdraw from the pope’s jurisdiction, not only by arrogating it to oneself or failing to recognize it. This thesis is maintained by Father Bisig of the Fraternity of St. Peter (FSSP). He writes:[88]

“Schism occurs when consecrations are made with the intention of withdrawing from pontifical jurisdiction or from communion of the universal Church.”

But this understanding of schism is simply false. Raoul Naz writes:

Original: “Das Schisma […] setzt eine systematische und habituelle Weigerung der Abhängigkeit voraus. Der Ungehorsam ist oft nur eine vorübergehende Handlung, ohne dass ihr Urheber in irgendeiner Weise die Autorität des Gesetzes oder des Gesetzgebers bestreitet und sich ihr auf habituelle Weise entziehen will.”

Translation: “Schism […] presupposes a systematic and habitual refusal of dependence. Disobedience is often only a passing act, without its author in any way disputing the authority of the law or of the lawgiver or wishing to withdraw from it habitually.”

(Dictionnaire de droit canonique, Paris: Letouzey et Ané, 1965, vol. VII, col. 886, quoted in [89]) St. Thomas writes:[90]

“The essence of schism consists in rebelliously disobeying the commandments: and I say”rebelliously,” since a schismatic both obstinately scorns the commandments of the Church, and refuses to submit to her judgment.”

and:[91]

Original: “Zum zweiten Punkt ist zu sagen, dass Schisma eine Spaltung bedeutet, die der Einheit der Liebe entgegensteht: Denn Schismatiker sind jene, die keine Harmonie in den Bräuchen der Kirche wahren, wie etwa den Gehorsam gegenüber den Prälaten der Kirche, und die eine eigene Kirche für sich errichten wollen: Und diese haben nicht von Anfang an eine perverse Lehre, sondern weichen vom Fundament der Kirche ab, wenden sich eitlem Gerede zu, erfinden allerlei Verirrungen und gleiten so schließlich in die Häresie ab; daher sagt Hieronymus, dass sich Häresie und Schisma wie Gattung und Art unterscheiden.”

Translation: “To the second point it must be said that schism means a division opposed to the unity of charity: for schismatics are those who do not preserve harmony in the practices of the Church, such as obedience to the prelates of the Church, and who want to set up a church of their own for themselves. And these do not have a perverse doctrine from the beginning, but depart from the foundation of the Church, turn to vain talk, invent all kinds of errors, and thus finally slip into heresy; hence Jerome says that heresy and schism differ as genus and species.”

Cajetan writes:

Original: “Ungehorsam wie hartnäckig auch immer konstituiert kein Schisma, außer es handelt sich um eine Rebellion gegen das Amt des Papstes oder das der Kirche, sodass man sich weigert ihm untertan zu sein, ihn als oberen anzuerkennen”

Translation: “Disobedience, however stubborn, does not constitute schism unless it is a rebellion against the office of the pope or that of the Church, such that one refuses to be subject to him and to recognize him as superior.”

The FSSPX does none of these things with its consecrations. It does not reject the authority of the pope in principle and does not rebel against it, but resists it only in certain cases. It does not refuse to recognize him as superior. Nor does it intend to build a parallel church of its own, as is shown by the fact that it does not arrogate jurisdiction to its bishops. Nor, unlike the Eastern “Orthodox” schismatics, has the FSSPX ever slipped into heresy. That schism is based on the fundamental rejection of papal authority is implicitly admitted also by John Paul II when, after the episcopal consecrations of 1988, he writes in Ecclesia Dei:[92]

“Hence such disobedience - which implies in practice the rejection of the Roman primacy - constitutes a schismatic act.”

But he is wrong in assuming that such an act implies rejection of the Roman primacy, as we have already discussed.

6.4. Does This Not Result in Excommunication?

For this one must first know the distinction between latae sententiae and ferendae sententiae. The former is a declaratory judgment, in which it is only established that a judgment has occurred; the latter is a constitutive judgment, that is, the judge’s sentence itself constitutes the judgment. An analogy would be a judge who establishes that a person was already convicted two years ago; here he can err, because it is only the establishment of a fact. But if he says, “You are convicted,” he cannot err, since his statement itself is the judgment.

6.4.1. The Excommunications of 1988

The offense on which the Vatican decree relied was Canon 1382 CIC 1983, today Canon 1387:[93]

Original: “Ein Bischof, der jemanden ohne päpstlichen Auftrag zum Bischof weiht, und ebenso, wer von ihm die Weihe empfängt, ziehen sich die dem Apostolischen Stuhl vorbehaltene Exkommunikation als Tatstrafe zu.”

Translation: “A bishop who consecrates someone a bishop without a pontifical mandate, and likewise the person who receives the consecration from him, incurs a latae sententiae excommunication reserved to the Apostolic See.”

The declaratory decree of Cardinal Gantin of July 1, 1988 reads in its English original:

“I declare that the above-mentioned Monsignor Marcel Lefebvre, and Bernard Fellay, Bernard Tissier de Mallerais, Richard Williamson and Alfonso de Galarreta have incurred ipso facto excommunication latae sententiae reserved to the Apostolic See.”

The verb “have incurred” is declaratory, not constitutive. John Paul II formulates it similarly in the motu proprio Ecclesia Dei of July 2, 1988:[94]

Original: “Sind Msgr. Lefebvre und die Priester Bernard Fellay, Bernard Tissier de Mallerais, Richard Williamson und Alfonso de Galarreta der schweren Strafe der Exkommunikation verfallen, wie die kirchliche Disziplin vorsieht.”

Translation: “Msgr. Lefebvre and the priests Bernard Fellay, Bernard Tissier de Mallerais, Richard Williamson, and Alfonso de Galarreta have incurred the grave penalty of excommunication, as ecclesiastical discipline provides.”

Precisely this declaratory character is the starting point of the canonical defense. Canon 1323 CIC 1983 provides:[95]

Original: “Keiner Strafe unterworfen ist, wer bei Übertretung eines Gesetzes oder eines Verwaltungsbefehls: […] 4° aus schwerer Furcht, wenngleich nur relativ schwer, gezwungen oder aufgrund einer Notlage oder erheblicher Beschwernis gehandelt hat, sofern jedoch die Tat nicht in sich schlecht ist oder zum Schaden der Seelen gereicht; […] 7° ohne Verschulden geglaubt hat, einen der in nn. 4 oder 5 genannten Umstände liege vor.”

Translation: “No one is subject to a penalty who, when violating a law or administrative precept: […] 4° was forced by grave fear, even if only relatively grave, or acted by reason of necessity or grave inconvenience, provided however that the act is not intrinsically evil or harmful to souls; […] 7° without fault believed that one of the circumstances mentioned in nn. 4 or 5 was present.”

Lefebvre explicitly invoked precisely these norms for the consecrations and quoted an opinion by Prof. Georg May, president of the Canon Law Seminar at the University of Mainz:[96]

“Thus you will find among these flyers which are put at your disposal, an admirable study done by Professor Georg May […] who marvelously explains why we are in a case of necessity: necessity to come and help your souls, to help you!”

The necessity to which Lefebvre refers can be named concretely: he expected his imminent death and had the impression that the Vatican was deliberately delaying consent to an episcopal consecration until after his death in order to bring the SSPX back under the influence of pro-conciliar forces afterward. This conviction at least fulfills the element of relatively grave fear in the sense of Canon 1323 n. 4. Whether this assessment is objectively correct is, however, secondary for the canonical question, since Canon 1323 n. 7 already establishes unculpable error about the existence of the state of necessity as a complete ground excluding penalty. Canon 1324 §1 n. 8 goes even further, according to which the penalty is diminished for one:[97]

Original: “der irrtümlich, wenngleich schuldhaft, geglaubt hat, es läge einer der in can. 1323, nn. 4 oder 5 genannten Umstände vor.”

Translation: “who erroneously, even if culpably, believed that one of the circumstances mentioned in can. 1323, nn. 4 or 5 was present.”

The decisive step follows from Canon 1324 §3:

Original: “Unter den in §1 genannten Umständen trifft den Täter keine Tatstrafe.”

Translation: “In the circumstances mentioned in §1, the offender is not subject to any latae sententiae penalty.”

The canonical commentary on this passage states:[98]

“§3 articulates a general principle that every diminution of imputability frees from latae sententiae penalties otherwise demanding full imputability.”

Since every mitigating ground of Canon 1324 §1 automatically excludes every latae sententiae penalty according to §3, and since Canon 1323 n. 7 qualifies unculpable error and Canon 1324 §1 n. 8 even culpable error about the existence of a state of necessity as sufficient, the legal conclusion is this: even if no objective state of necessity existed, Lefebvre and the four bishops incurred no latae sententiae penalty as long as they were subjectively convinced of a state of necessity without bad faith. The American canonist Fr. Gerard Murray, whose licentiate thesis at the Pontifical Gregorian University received the highest grade, comes to the conclusion that the latae sententiae excommunication against Lefebvre, de Castro Mayer, and the four bishops was “not valid according to strict canonical law,” and that the associated accusation of schism was “not valid in the formal sense.”[99]

6.4.2. Ferendae sententiae Excommunication

But what if the FSSPX bishops are excommunicated ferendae sententiae in 2026? If the justification of the FSSPX that I have defended up to this point is correct, then that would simply be an unjust, though valid, judgment. The recognized Dictionnaire de théologie catholique writes:

Original: „Die Theologen des Mittelalters, wenigstens die des 14., 15. und 16. Jahrhunderts, tragen Sorge festzustellen, daß das Schisma eine illegitime Trennung von der Kirche ist; denn, so sagen sie, es könnte auch eine legitime Trennung geben, so wenn etwa jemand dem Papst den Gehorsam verweigerte, weil dieser ihm eine schlechte oder ungehörige Sache beföhle […] Die Überlegung mag überflüssig erscheinen, und man kann denken, daß hier, wie im Fall der ungerechten Exkommunikation, eine rein äußere und scheinbare Trennung von der Einheit vorliegt.“

Translation: “The theologians of the Middle Ages, at least those of the fourteenth, fifteenth, and sixteenth centuries, take care to establish that schism is an illegitimate separation from the Church; for, they say, there could also be a legitimate separation, as for example if someone refused obedience to the pope because the pope commanded him to do something bad or improper […] The reflection may seem superfluous, and one may think that here, as in the case of unjust excommunication, there is a purely external and apparent separation from unity.”

(quoted in 2. Vatikanisches Konzil: Verdunklung der Kirche, p. 264) And St. Bellarmine writes:

Original: “Ich antworte, daß […] ein solcher [nämlich der ungerecht Exkommunizierte] sich aufgrund äußerer Gemeinschaft innerhalb der Kirche befindet, die den Menschen im eigentlichen Sinne zum Glied jener sichtbaren Kirche macht.”

Translation: “I answer that […] such a person [namely the unjustly excommunicated] is within the Church by reason of external communion, which makes a man a member of that visible Church in the proper sense.”

(quoted in 2. Vatikanisches Konzil: Verdunklung der Kirche, p. 267)

7. Overall Conclusion

Original: “Wenn aber wir oder gar ein Engel vom Himmel euch ein anderes Evangelium verkünden als das, das wir euch verkündet haben - er sei verflucht!” (Gal 1,8)

Translation: “But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach to you a different gospel from the one we preached to you - let him be accursed!” (Gal 1:8)

Saint Paul establishes a principle[100] that carries the whole defense presented here. The binding force of ecclesiastical authority is not limitless, but bound to the truth it serves. So much so that even an apostle or an angel from heaven would fall under anathema if he preached another gospel. This precise relationship between truth and authority is the standard by which the resistance of the FSSPX must be measured, and by which, as I have tried to show, it is justified. The starting point was the diagnosis of the crisis. Modernism, condemned by Pius X as the “synthesis of all heresies,” reduces, in the interpretation defended here, the world to the realm of becoming and denies the realm of being, from which follows relativism in doctrine, liturgy, and morals. This crisis is not hypothetical. It can be seen in measurable indicators: in the loss of faith regarding transubstantiation, in the voting behavior of whole episcopal conferences, in the state of belief among the clergy according to studies, and in the absence of any canonical reaction to publicly Modernist teaching. Because Modernism thus systematically endangers the salvation of souls, and because salus animarum suprema lex applies, the same basic question arises for the three points of FSSPX resistance - the refusal regarding V2, regarding the Novus Ordo, and the episcopal consecrations without mandate: whether fidelity to the received truth not only permits but demands disobedience toward current discipline. On the first point, it was shown that the criticized passages of V2 were not proclaimed infallibly but belong to authentic, non-infallible magisterium, toward which dissent is possible under qualified conditions. This follows from the basic principle that a doctrine which is in principle capable of error and whose truth content is naturally knowable cannot oblige contradictionless assent, and it is expressly confirmed by pre-conciliar manual theology in Ott and Van Noort. That Dignitatis Humanae, Lumen Gentium VIII, and Lumen Gentium XVI contradict received doctrine in their natural reading was demonstrated in detail; and by the example of Saint Maximus, who presumed good intention in Honorius but nevertheless would never have signed a formula readable as heretical, it was shown that good intention does not annul the objective statement of a text. The section on the heresies justified by V2 - from the alleged continuance of the Old Covenant, through the condemned ecumenism of return, to participation in non-Catholic services - further showed how grave the crisis is and how little the faithful today can rely on receiving unadulterated doctrine from their shepherds. On the second point, Father Gaudron’s formal argument was refined in the moderate position. The Novus Ordo is bad in itself as a rite, not because every Mass celebrated according to it would be bad, but because, as a rite, it leaves too much room to obscure the sacrificial character and thereby fails privatively to fulfill an essential task of the Mass. Since the law prescribing such a rite does not serve the common good of the Church, it does not bind in conscience according to the principle lex injusta non obligat. For the FSSPX there is the additional point that celebrating the Novus Ordo would mean revoking a substantively never-refuted criticism and would thereby spiritually harm those faithful who rely on its seriousness: a scandalum pusillorum in the sense of Saint Thomas. On the third and most serious point, the episcopal consecrations, several independent arguments converge. The prohibition is not divine law, for episcopal consecration does not necessarily confer jurisdiction, as the classic cases of auxiliary, titular, and retired bishops show, so that the prohibition is divine law only insofar as someone arrogates universal jurisdiction to himself, which the FSSPX precisely does not do. Since it is therefore merely positive Church law, two meta-juridical principles apply. Epikie provides a good, though not coercive, argument, because the original legislator enacted the prohibition against the usurpation of the episcopal office by secular powers, not against the internal Church emergency in which the FSSPX intends precisely to preserve the received order. The decisive argument is Necessitas, the priority of the highest law, the salvation of souls, over positive law. EDA’s determination that no state of necessity existed is not an infallible doctrinal judgment but a fallible factual determination outside the limits of infallibility drawn by Pastor Aeternus, as shown by the cases of Athanasius and Eusebius of Dorylaeum, whose independent resistance was later explicitly confirmed by the Church. The objection of private jurisdiction fails because the FSSPX proves by its very self-limitation that it claims no comprehensive power of disposal over the laws. The consecrations are also not schismatic, because schism according to Naz, Thomas, and Cajetan presupposes a fundamental, habitual rejection of papal authority, which the FSSPX does not enact; and finally, they incur no valid automatic penalty because according to Canons 1323 and 1324, even unculpable error, indeed even culpable error, about the existence of a state of necessity excludes latae sententiae excommunication. The decisive question remains whether the path of the FSSPX really was the right one, or whether a less drastic path would have sufficed. The answer is that no such path exists. The Ecclesia Dei communities preserve the traditional liturgy, but they are bound to full acceptance of V2 and cannot publicly name the problematic texts as contradicting Tradition. Whoever wants to fight the crisis at its root must be able to do precisely that. Silent resistance within the existing structures fails even more fundamentally because it cannot be extended to priestly formation, and Tradition therefore breaks off over two generations. Without independent seminaries and without bishops who supervise these seminaries and ordain new priests, the resistance has no future. The episcopal consecrations of 1988 were therefore not an excess, but the proportionally minimal means that made the resistance reproducible beyond the death of its founder. This is precisely the meaning of the Pauline principle with which this conclusion began. Where an authority, however high, effectively favors another gospel, disobedience is not infidelity; obedience is. The FSSPX has not left the Church; in a state of necessity, with the least means sufficient for the task, it preserved what the Church has always taught and celebrated. In this, and not in rebellion against the Petrine office, lies the justification of its path and of its episcopal consecrations.

8. Further Relevant Points

Here I want to touch on a few further relevant points that are not necessary for the arguments presented, but should answer some questions that may arise.

8.1. Van Noort on Disciplinary Infallibility

A critic might appeal, against several of the previous arguments, especially against the Necessitas argument and against the NO section, to the manualist teaching on the Church’s secondary infallibility regarding general discipline. This teaching deserves its own treatment, because its most prominent representative, Msgr. Gerard van Noort (1861-1946), carries great weight as one of the leading pre-conciliar dogmatic theologians. Van Noort’s three-volume Tractatus apologetici et dogmatici was for decades a standard textbook in the seminaries of the universal Church; Pius XI appointed him domestic prelate in 1934 and gave him a Roman honorary doctorate; and Msgr. Joseph Clifford Fenton, the most prominent defender of traditional theology under Pius XII, counted him among the indispensable manualists of the twentieth century. Whoever simply brushes Van Noort aside therefore fails not only to meet the burden of proof, but makes precisely the mistake the Modernists always accused manual theology of making. In Christ’s Church (Dogmatic Theology Vol. II, ed. Castelot/Murphy, Newman Press 1957, Assertion 3, pp. 113-115), Van Noort maintains as theologically certain the thesis that the Church’s infallibility also extends to general discipline:

“The Church’s infallibility extends to the general discipline of the Church. This proposition is theologically certain. By the term ‘general discipline of the Church’ are meant those ecclesiastical laws passed for the universal Church for the direction of Christian worship and Christian living.”

On careful reading, however, this thesis supports our argument rather than contradicting it, because Van Noort precisely restricts its scope in three successive steps. First, within every disciplinary law he distinguishes two judgments, one doctrinal and one practical:

“When the Church’s rulers sanction a law, they implicitly make a twofold judgment: 1. ‘This law squares with the Church’s doctrine of faith and morals’; that is, it imposes nothing that is at odds with sound belief and good morals. This amounts to a doctrinal decree. 2. ‘This law, considering all the circumstances, is most opportune.’ This is a decree of practical judgment.” (ibid., p. 114)

Only the first judgment concerns secondary infallibility. The second, the practical judgment, Van Noort expressly excludes from infallibility:

“Although it would be rash to cast aspersions on the timeliness of a law, especially at the very moment when the Church imposes or expressly reaffirms it, still the Church does not claim to be infallible in issuing a decree of practical judgment. For the Church’s rulers were never promised the highest degree of prudence for the conduct of affairs.” (ibid., p. 114, emphasis here)

Second, even the positive scope of his thesis is narrowly defined. What it excludes are universal laws that are “at odds with faith or morality” or “by its very nature conducive to the injury of souls” (ibid., p. 114). As historical evidence, Van Noort cites the condemnation of the Jansenist Synod of Pistoia by Pius VI in Auctorem fidei (1794), where the following is rejected:

“that the Church could establish discipline which would be dangerous, harmful, and conducive to superstition and materialism” (DH 2602).

The class of examples by which Van Noort’s thesis is oriented is therefore active falsification, such as discipline that leads to superstition or materialism, not every form of inexpediency or privative deficiency. Third, and this is already classically embedded in Thomistic law, the class of non-binding unjust laws according to ST I-II q.96 a.4 is significantly broader than the class of laws that Van Noort protects from false ecclesial sanction. Thomas lists, as conditions for lex injusta, among other things failure to promote the common good, exceeding the lawgiver’s authority, and unequal distribution. Van Noort’s protection, by contrast, applies only when a law actively harms by its nature. A law can fail sufficiently to promote the common good of the Church and therefore not bind the conscience as lex injusta without falling into the narrow class prohibited by Van Noort. When we apply this to our previous argument, two clear findings emerge. On the one hand, the Necessitas argument fits seamlessly into Van Noort’s schema. The statement in EDA that no state of necessity exists is not the doctrinal assertion “episcopal consecrations without mandate are incompatible with faith and morals,” but the practical judgment “in the circumstances of 1988 there is no state of necessity that would justify such consecrations.” Van Noort himself removes such judgments from infallibility. Thus, from traditional manual theology, he confirms exactly what we derived above from Pastor Aeternus and Ott. On the other hand, the NO arguments also do not come into conflict with Van Noort, provided they are read correctly in Thomistic terms. The thesis of this document is not that the NO is actively heretical or superstitious, but that it privatively fails to fulfill its task of clearly expressing the essence of the Mass, and that the law prescribing it does not sufficiently serve the common good of the Church. Both claims lie categorically outside Van Noort’s protected scope. Privative deficiency in the sense of the Thomistic determination of evil as privatio of the due good is conceptually different from by its very nature conducive to the injury of souls, which is what Van Noort means and what he exemplifies by the condemnation of Pistoia. The only point at which the NO threatened to become actively heretical in the strict sense was the original definition in IGMR 7. Precisely there, however, it was corrected shortly after promulgation, which incidentally speaks rather against the assumption that the Magisterium universally sanctioned a law harmful by its very nature. Whoever wants to refute the NO argument by appeal to Van Noort would therefore have to show that the NO as a rite is by its very nature conducive to the injury of souls. But that is precisely not claimed in this document. What is claimed is that the NO is privatively deficient and does not sufficiently serve the common good of the Church. Both are logically compatible with Van Noort, because the doctrine of lex injusta in Thomas recognizes a broader class of non-binding laws than that narrow class in which Van Noort sees the Church protected from false universal sanction. As Van Noort himself emphasizes in the corollary to Assertion 3, disciplinary infallibility harmonizes:

“beautifully with the mutability of even universal laws. For a law, even though it be thoroughly consonant with revealed truth, can, given a change in circumstances, become less timely or even useless, so that prudence may dictate its abrogation or modification.” (ibid., p. 114)

For Van Noort, the mutability even of universal laws according to prudence is not a contradiction of his infallibility thesis, but its natural complement. Thus the position of manual theology, represented by its authoritative witness, is not only compatible with the arguments of this document, but supports them at decisive points.

8.2. Have the Gates of Hell Prevailed Over the Church?

An objection brought against every form of crisis diagnosis is this: if the crisis is really as grave as I have claimed above, and if the FSSPX really must resort to episcopal consecrations against current law, then Christ’s promise seems to lose its force.

Original: “Du bist Petrus, der Fels, und auf diesen Felsen werde ich meine Kirche bauen, und die Mächte der Unterwelt werden sie nicht überwältigen” (Mt 16,18).

Translation: “You are Peter, the rock, and upon this rock I will build my Church, and the powers of the underworld will not prevail against it” (Mt 16:18).

Whoever assumes a crisis of this magnitude, so the objection goes, would have to admit that hell has prevailed. What the promise guarantees must be stated precisely. It does not guarantee that the Church will suffer no grave crises, but that she will not perish and that her faith will not be extinguished. A victory of hell would be the extinction of the Church or the complete extinguishing of the received faith. Neither is manifestly the case today. The received faith is handed on in the FSSPX, in other traditional communities, in faithful bishops, priests, and laity; the Holy See is occupied; the Sacrament of the Altar is celebrated in countless churches. The question is therefore not whether the gates of hell have prevailed, but whether a crisis of this type and this magnitude is compatible with the promise. A look at Church history answers precisely this question negatively, that is, such crises have in fact already been passed through repeatedly without the promise being considered broken. On the doctrinal axis, the Arian crisis of the fourth century is the most impressive case. At the parallel synods of Rimini and Seleucia (359), under political pressure from Emperor Constantius II, most western and eastern bishops signed an Arian or semi-Arian-readable formula in which the homoousios was abandoned. Jerome summarizes the situation in a sentence that has since become proverbial (Jerome, Dialogus contra Luciferianos 19, PL 23, 181, Latin full text: [101]; English translation: [102]):

Original: “Tunc usiae nomen abolitum est: tunc Nicaenae fidei damnatio conclamata est. Ingemuit totus orbis, et Arianum se esse miratus est.”

Translation: “Then the word usia was abolished; then the condemnation of the Nicene faith was proclaimed. The whole world groaned and was amazed to find itself Arian.”

Athanasius at times stood practically alone against a large part of the episcopate, Athanasius contra mundum, and this with the explicit approval of Tradition, which later honored him as a Doctor of the Church. Liberius I have already treated above; he belongs to the same line. The crisis stretched from Nicaea (325) to Constantinople I (381), that is, over more than half a century. No one concluded from it that the gates of hell had prevailed. On the axis of papal error, beyond the cases already treated of Honorius and Nicholas I, the case of John XXII is especially significant. Between 1331 and 1334, in several sermons at Avignon, he taught that the souls of the just do not behold God immediately after death, but only after the general resurrection. Theologians publicly contradicted him; on the eve of his death, John XXII retracted his position in the bull Ne super his. His successor Benedict XII defined the opposite de fide in 1336 in the dogmatic constitution Benedictus Deus (Benedict XII, Benedictus Deus, January 29, 1336, DH 1000-1002, Latin full text on vatican.va: [103]; English translation: [104]):

Original: “animae sanctorum omnium… mox post mortem ipsorum… fuerunt, sunt et erunt in caelo,… viderunt et vident divinam essentiam visione intuitiva et etiam faciali”

Translation: “the souls of all the saints… immediately after their death… have been, are, and will be in heaven,… they have seen and see the divine essence by intuitive and even face-to-face vision”

A reigning pope publicly preached against the later de fide definition, retracted it himself, and his successor dogmatized the opposite. Here too no one concluded from the event that Christ’s promise had been invalidated. On the moral axis, the situation in Church history has several times sunk so low that today’s scandals recede behind it. In the Saeculum obscurum (ca. 904-964), the papacy at times became the plaything of Roman families. Sergius III ascended the throne in 904 after his predecessor Leo V and the antipope Christopher had allegedly been murdered at his instigation. Stephen VI’s Synodus horrenda, the Cadaver Synod of 897, had the exhumed corpse of Pope Formosus brought to trial in pontifical vestments, posthumously condemned, had three fingers of his right hand cut off, and thrown into the Tiber. John XII was deposed in 963 by a synod in Rome convened by Otto I for murder, perjury, incest, simony, idolatry, and a long list of further crimes; the detailed account is given by the eyewitness Liutprand of Cremona in his Historia Ottonis, chapters 4-12 (English translation with source reference: [105]). Benedict IX sold the papacy in 1045 to his godfather, who then reigned as Gregory VI, and later returned once more as a claimant. The Renaissance papacy under Alexander VI Borgia (1492-1503) continued with open illegitimate children, dynastic nepotism, and political murder campaigns. In none of these cases did anyone conclude from the scandal that the gates of hell had prevailed or that the sacraments were henceforth invalid. On the axis of visible unity, the Western Schism (1378-1417) is the sharpest comparison. After the death of Gregory XI, Urban VI was first elected in Rome in 1378; then the same cardinals, claiming their own election invalid because of pressure from the Roman populace, elected Clement VII in Avignon. Both claimed the papacy. In 1409 the Council of Pisa attempted to end the division by declaring both deposed and electing Alexander V; since those deposed did not abandon their claims, after that there were three simultaneous claimants to the papacy. Geographically, Christendom was roughly divided as follows: before Pisa, France, Scotland, Castile, Aragon, Sicily, Naples, and the Papal States adhered to the Avignon line; the Empire, England, Italy outside the south, Scandinavia, Poland, and Hungary to the Roman line. After Pisa, France, England, Portugal, Bohemia, Prussia, and parts of Italy and the Empire switched to the Pisan line; Spain and Scotland remained with Avignon; Naples, Poland, Bavaria, and parts of the Empire remained with Rome (cf. Wikipedia, Western Schism, [106], and Council of Pisa, [107]). The Annuario Pontificio today recognizes the Roman line, that is, Urban VI, Boniface IX, Innocent VII, and Gregory XII, as legitimate. On the basis of established population estimates for the late Middle Ages (Western Christendom overall about 45-50 million, France alone 12-15 million, the Empire 10-12 million, the Iberian Peninsula 5-6 million), the adherents can be roughly quantified: before Pisa, an estimated 40 to 45% of Latin Christendom adhered to the Avignon line, today regarded as illegitimate, and about 50 to 55% to the Roman. After Pisa, the ratios turned drastically: the Pisan line, also regarded today as illegitimate, was recognized by about 55 to 60% of Christendom, the Avignon line by about 10 to 15%, so that only about 15 to 20% remained with the line today recognized as the only legitimate Roman line. This means concretely: at the height of the confusion, roughly four out of five Catholics adhered to popes who are today classified as antipopes. Saints stood on different sides: Catherine of Siena supported the Roman line, Vincent Ferrer at first the Avignon line and changed only late. At that time it was epistemically certain for no one to know who the true pope was. Here too no one concluded from this enormous confusion that the gates of hell had prevailed. On the contrary, the crisis was overcome in 1417 at the Council of Constance with the election of Martin V. Thus every aspect of the present crisis that the objector would have to classify as unprecedented is covered by a historical precedent. Doctrinal pressure across the hierarchy: the Arian crisis. Papal error in a doctrinally relevant question: Honorius, Nicholas I, John XXII. Moral corruption at the top: Saeculum obscurum, Renaissance papacy. Long duration: the Arian crisis more than half a century, the Schism 39 years, the pornocratic age around 60 years. Massive confusion over the lawful line and factual loyalty of majorities to antipopes: the Schism. If none of these cases counted as a victory of the gates of hell, then the present state does not either. In addition, a pattern stands out from Church history: every severe crisis was historically followed by a phase of purification and dogmatic clarification in which the Church emerged strengthened from the upheaval. Constantinople I (381) followed Arianism with the definitive establishment of Trinitarian dogmatics; Benedictus Deus (1336) followed John XXII with the dogmatic clarification of the immediate vision of God; the Cluniac and later Gregorian Reform followed the Saeculum obscurum; the Council of Constance (1417) followed the Schism with reunification; the Council of Trent (1545-1563) followed the Reformation with the Counter-Reformation renewal; Vatican I (1869-1870) followed Gallicanism, Enlightenment, and Revolution with the dogmatic definition of the Petrine office. This is the pattern in which Christ’s promise historically realizes itself: not by avoiding crises, but by preserving the faith through them, often through small remnants, followed by a renewal of the whole Church. Mt 16:18 is therefore not invalidated. On the contrary, that in the midst of the present crisis traditional bishops are consecrated, that the received rite is celebrated in countless places, that religious communities, seminaries, and families hand on the Catholic faith in full, is itself evidence that the gates of hell have not prevailed over the Church. Whoever wants to infer from the gravity of the crisis a victory of hell would have to do the same, with equal consistency, for the Arian crisis, the Saeculum obscurum, and the Western Schism, which no one has ever done. The promise holds, but it holds in the mode of testing in crisis and purification, not as a guarantee of a frictionless course.

8.3. But the FSSPX Has No Jurisdiction

The FSSPX indeed has no ordinary jurisdiction and has never claimed to have it. This lack of jurisdiction endangers only the liceity of the sacraments of marriage and confession. In these cases the principle ecclesia supplet applies, according to which in an emergency the Church supplies the necessary jurisdiction. That a state of emergency exists we have already seen above.

8.4. Sedevacantism

The critical statements I made above about V2 could give the impression that my position ultimately leads to sedevacantism. If the popes of the post-conciliar period teach or tolerate heresies, and if a heretical pope according to traditional doctrine is no longer pope, then it seems logically to follow that the sees of Rome have been vacant since V2. I do not draw this conclusion, and the reason lies in a distinction firmly rooted in Church history. The thesis that a heretical pope loses his office goes back, in the form most often cited today, to Bellarmine. In De Romano Pontifice lib. II, cap. 30 he writes (full text: [108], cf. [109]):

Original: “Quinta opinio vera est: Papa haereticus manifestus per se desinit esse Papa et caput, sicut per se desinit esse Christianus et membrum corporis Ecclesiae: quare ab Ecclesia iudicari et puniri potest.”

Translation: “The fifth opinion is true: a manifestly heretical pope ceases of himself to be pope and head, just as he ceases of himself to be a Christian and member of the body of the Church; wherefore he can be judged and punished by the Church.”

Bellarmine precisely limits this thesis in the same chapter. He speaks exclusively of the haereticus manifestus, the publicly professing heretic, and expressly excludes the hidden heretic, who remains a member of the Church and therefore pope. Mere suspicion, error, or a misunderstood word therefore precisely do not suffice. What is decisive for applying this doctrine to a concrete case is the canonical-theological distinction between material and formal heresy. One is materially heretical if one maintains a statement that is objectively incompatible with Catholic teaching without being conscious of the contradiction or without, after sufficient correction, persisting in one’s error with pertinacious obstinacy. One is formally heretical only if one denies a revealed truth with full knowledge and precisely such pertinacity. Bellarmine’s thesis presupposes formal heresy, because otherwise the condition of consciously leaving the Church is not fulfilled. Whoever merely errs materially has not separated himself from the Church, but remains in a state of teachability. This distinction is not academic, but has a prominent Church-historical application that is structurally parallel to the present situation. Pope Honorius I (625-638), in his letter to Patriarch Sergius, used the formula that in Christ there is one will (unam voluntatem), and the Third Council of Constantinople condemned him posthumously in 681 in Session XIII as a supporter of Monothelitism. It was Maximus the Confessor, one of the most important saints and confessor-martyrs of the Eastern Church, who defended Honorius against the accusation of formal heresy. In his letter to the priest Marinus of Cyprus (ca. 641), Maximus argues that by the formula one will Honorius had merely meant that Christ had no conflicting fleshly will of concupiscence, since He was conceived and born without sin. Maximus relies for this on the witness of John Symponus, the secretary who had drafted the letter for Honorius (cf. Catholic Encyclopedia, “St. Maximus of Constantinople,” [110]). In a later letter to the patrician Peter, Maximus even calls Honorius “the great Honorius” and places him in line with his unquestionably orthodox successors Severinus and John IV. What Maximus does here, precisely, is to mark the difference between a materially ambiguous formulation and formal heresy. Honorius used words readable in a heretical sense, but he did not mean them with heretical intent. I follow exactly this distinction with regard to the post-conciliar popes. They approved or taught texts that, in one reading, contradict received doctrine, but they did not set themselves in pertinacious obstinacy against better-known Catholic truth; rather, they operate within a theological framework in which they subjectively believe themselves to be compatible with Tradition. A second precedent supports this. Pope Nicholas I (858-867) declared in his Responsa ad consulta Bulgarorum of November 866, cap. 104 (full text: [111]; also Mansi XV, 401-434):

Original: “Si vero in nomine sanctae Trinitatis vel tantum in nomine Christi, sicut in actibus apostolorum legimus (…) baptizati sunt, (…) constat eos non esse denuo baptizandos.”

Translation: “But if they were baptized in the name of the Holy Trinity or only in the name of Christ, as we read in the Acts of the Apostles (…), it is clear that they are not to be baptized again.”

A baptism only in the name of Christ is therefore equivalent to the Trinitarian form and is not to be repeated. This statement contradicts the later teaching that the Trinitarian form is prescribed necessitate sacramenti, as the Council of Florence held in 1439 in the Decretum pro Armenis:

Original: “Forma autem est: Ego te baptizo in nomine Patris et Filii et Spiritus sancti.” (DH 1314)

Translation: “The form, however, is: I baptize you in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.” (DH 1314)

Nicholas I therefore erred in a doctrinally relevant question. But no one in Church history has ever claimed that he therefore lost the Petrine office or was not a true pope. He is still venerated today as Saint Nicholas the Great; all subsequent popes considered his official acts valid; his cardinal appointments remained uncontested. This shows that a material error in the non-infallible papal magisterium does not abolish the papacy as such. Otherwise one would have to deny the pontificate not only of Nicholas I, but also the validity of all appointments and ordinations he performed, with consequences for apostolic succession that no one has ever drawn. Thus the concern for the visibility of the Church is not endangered either. Pastor Aeternus chapter 2 (DS 3056-3058) teaches de fide that the Petrine office endures perpetually in the Roman Pontiffs, and Christ Himself promised to be with His Church “all days, even to the end of the world” (Mt 28:20). Pius XII emphasizes in Mystici Corporis Christi (1943, AAS 35, 199-248) the indestructible visibility of the one Church of Christ. If the popes since V2 err only materially and not formally, then they remain true popes, the Petrine office remains occupied, and the visibility of the Church is preserved. There is no hidden sedevacantist state that would collide with this doctrine. But this does not reinstate the duty to accept every non-infallible papal teaching. Materially heretical statements, or statements objectively incompatible with received doctrine, remain substantively false, regardless of whether their author himself holds them pertinaciously or not. I may therefore at the same time recognize that the post-conciliar popes are true popes and refuse to assent to those statements that depart from the objective finding of Tradition. This precise double attitude is the position of the FSSPX. It is not sedevacantism, because it does not deny the papacy; nor is it Modernist accommodation, because it places truth above the claimed authority. It therefore follows precisely the model Maximus the Confessor showed in the case of Honorius, and it is fully compatible with the dogmatic teaching on the visibility and perpetuity of the Church.[112]

8.5. Proportionality

After all the arguments in this document, only the prudential objection remains that episcopal consecration, as the most drastic and canonically sanctioned step, exceeded what was proportionally necessary, because the survival of Tradition did not depend on the bishops of precisely this community. This objection misunderstands what proportionality means in a state of necessity. What is required is not the mildest conceivable means as such, but the mildest means still sufficient to remedy the necessity. A means that does not remedy the necessity is not a milder alternative, but simply not an alternative. That is exactly the case with the non-episcopal paths. The diagnosed necessity is not a punctual event like the starving man’s theft in Thomas, but a structural crisis extending over generations. But priests can be ordained only by a bishop, and priests die. Without a bishop who shares the diagnosis and ordains men trained in the critique, the entire resistance ends with the death of the last bishop favorable to it. A resistance without its own bishops is therefore not a more measured resistance, but a resistance with a built-in expiration date. The consecrations were therefore not greater drasticness, but the minimum capable of withstanding the duration and nature of the necessity at all. This also shifts the burden of proof: the critic would not have to show that a milder path was conceivable, but that it would have sufficed. The appeal to the claim that God will preserve Tradition by other means in any case does not help the objection. This argument proves too much, for by the same logic no human effort to preserve the faith would ever be necessary: Athanasius too would not have had to resist, since God could have preserved the Nicene faith even without him. Providence, however, ordinarily works not past responsible secondary causes, but through them. The promise of Mt 16:18 guarantees the result, that the Church will not perish, but not the dispensability of any particular means, and it is not a free pass for inaction. In history it has always realized itself precisely through those remnants that acted in the crisis. The question of the one acting is therefore never the abstract one whether the Church will survive, but the concrete one whether this is the means available to him to avert a foreseeable grave loss. Added to this is the asymmetry of risks that guides every prudent decision under uncertainty. The loss of the episcopal line would have been irreversible in the relevant period: if the favorable bishops die without successors, one can no longer ordain, and the formation tradition breaks off. The costs of the consecrations, by contrast - the canonical penalties and the irregular status - were reversible, and in fact they were partially withdrawn when Benedict XVI lifted the excommunications in 2009 and Francis granted faculties for confession in 2015. Whoever must choose between a reversible harm and an irreversible loss chooses prudently when he prevents the irreversible loss. The later partial normalization confirmed precisely this: the harm incurred was healable; the loss avoided would not have been. Finally, that the mildest sufficient means was chosen, and not maximalism, is shown by the self-limitation of the consecrations themselves. Only the power of orders, indispensable for the reproduction of the priesthood, was taken, and precisely not the jurisdiction that would have made the bishops into a parallel hierarchy. Even within the drastic step, therefore, the minimal form was chosen. Proportionality is finally to be judged ex ante, according to the foresight reasonably possible for the one acting: a dying founder, a consent repeatedly delayed in his justified perception, and therefore the realistic expectation that the window would close with his death. A means granted only afterward would have been equivalent ex ante to no means being granted at all. More than prudential certainty cannot be reached here, because prudential judgments by their nature are not proofs. But the combination of these considerations - that every milder path would not have remedied the necessity, and that this insufficiency in fact later proved itself - compresses the remaining doubt very far.

[112] Even if one did not share the classification defended here of the post-conciliar popes as erring only materially, not formally, and instead assumed formal heresy, the argument would not necessarily lead to sedevacantism. In what follows I sketch my own admittedly novel solution to the problem, which I do not present as the standard position of the FSSPX, but as a reserve argument for the hypothetical case that someone did not want to apply the distinction between material and formal heresy to these popes. Structurally similar, though mechanically formulated differently, is the Cassiciacum thesis of Mgr. Guérard des Lauriers (1979, Cahiers de Cassiciacum), which distinguishes between a materialiter and a formaliter pope and has the same problem in view. Three propositions are in tension here. First, Bellarmine’s teaching that a manifest formally heretical pope loses his office ipso facto (De Romano Pontifice lib. II, cap. 30, https://www.fisheaters.com/bellarmine.html). Second, the de fide dogma of the visibility and permanence of the Church and of her Petrine office, formulated in Pastor Aeternus chapter 2 (DS 3056-3058), Mt 28:20, and Pius XII, Mystici Corporis Christi (1943, AAS 35, 199-248). Third, that according to Catholic doctrine a pope in formal heresy is not impossible; otherwise Bellarmine’s whole discussion would be without object. From the first and third propositions it seems to follow that a non-universally recognized vacancy of the See would be possible, which would violate the second proposition, for a hidden sedevacantist state with all practical consequences, from invalid cardinal appointments to the potential rupture of apostolic succession, would no longer allow the Church to remain visibly in being. My solution draws on the old theological principle ecclesia supplet, in German “kirchliche Suppletion der Jurisdiktion,” the Church’s supplying of jurisdiction. If an Orthodox or schismatic priest who formally stands outside the visible unity of the Church can validly and licitly administer sacraments to Catholics in danger of death, as Canon 844 §2 CIC/1983 expressly recognizes and as was already recognized in the theological manuals before the Code (cf. Suarez, De Censuris disp. XV; Cajetan, Commentaria in Summam Theologiae III, q. 64, a. 9; Prümmer, Manuale Theologiae Moralis III, n. 285), then this does not rest on a jurisdictional power still remaining in him himself, but on a supplementation by the Church herself ad necessitatem. Bellarmine’s statement that in the heretic there remains no spiritual power over those who remain in the Church (cited in De Rom. Pont. II, 30) refers to the heretic’s own jurisdiction, which he can no longer hold of himself, not to the possibility that the Church supplements his acts for the preservation of the higher good. Otherwise even the supply to the Orthodox priest would be excluded, which is obviously not ecclesial practice. The deeper principle is a combination of three elements. First, salus animarum suprema lex (Can. 1752 CIC/1983), the salvation of souls as the supreme law to which the positive canonical order is subordinated. Second, that the Petrine office is instituted propter homines, that is, that its purpose lies in the salvation of the faithful and not in the disposal of the officeholder; from this ordering to an end it follows, according to Suarez, that the Church can by her own power supply what is necessary to attain this end (De Censuris disp. XV). Third, the bonum commune Ecclesiae, which in an unprecedented ecclesial crisis requires the continued existence of visible structures. If the Church can fulfill her dogmatically guaranteed visible permanence only by supplying to a materially designated but, because of formal heresy, personally office-incapable pope the jurisdictional acts necessary for continuity, then this supply is not an arbitrary expansion of ecclesia supplet, but required by the basic principle itself. This supply is narrowly limited. It does not supplement the whole Magisterium with all its implications, but only those acts necessary for preserving visible continuity, namely cardinal appointments for the continuity of papal election, the ratification of valid episcopal consecrations, and valid liturgical promulgations. What is not supplied is teaching authority for heretical statements, because these contradict the purpose for whose sake the supply takes place at all. True statements of such an acting pope are to be accepted, but not by reason of his office, which he no longer holds of himself, but by reason of truth itself. The narrowly defined infallibility from Pastor Aeternus chapter 4 (DS 3074) remains untouched, moreover, because it follows from the infallibility of the Church and from the position as head, which is preserved through supply quoad effectum; a pope who errs only materially or is formally heretical will empirically avoid ex cathedra acts anyway, because their formal requirements - universal binding force and definitive character - collide with his inner disposition. The supply ends as soon as the state of emergency is ended. This occurs when the Church formally establishes the heretic as such and thus the visibility no longer has to be preserved through his person. This is not conciliarism, because a council does not set itself above a pope, but declaratively establishes that the man in question is ex hypothesi not pope. From this moment Bellarmine’s thesis ipso facto applies without a safety mechanism; the Church no longer supplies, because she can now fulfill her duty to visible continuity through a regular election. It is important for me to stress that this construction does not have to be invoked for the present situation of the FSSPX. The position I defended above holds the post-conciliar popes to be material, not formal, errants, in analogy to Maximus’s defense of Honorius. Thus Bellarmine’s condition is not triggered in the first place, and no supply is needed. The solution sketched here is intended only as a hypothetical reserve argument in case someone did not want to apply the material/formal distinction to these popes; it shows that even then sedevacantism does not necessarily follow. It is also my own thesis, which in this form does not belong to the standard repertoire of traditional theology, but remains structurally related to the Cassiciacum tradition and reconstructible from classical principles (salus animarum, propter homines, bonum commune Ecclesiae).
