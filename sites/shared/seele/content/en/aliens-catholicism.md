# Aliens and Catholicism

## The Fermi Paradox

In a certain sense, the Fermi paradox is an argument both for and against the existence of extraterrestrial life. It is named after the Italian-American physicist Enrico Fermi. In a 1950 conversation with other physicists he raised the apparent contradiction between the sheer size of the universe—which might lead us to expect intelligent life on planets other than Earth—and the fact that we have not observed any.

The second part, the absence of signs of extraterrestrial life, counts against the existence of aliens. The first part, the sheer size of the universe, counts in its favor. The latter is made more explicit in the so-called _Drake equation_. Named after the American astrophysicist Frank Drake, it estimates the likely number (N) of civilizations in the Milky Way:

$$
N = R_* \cdot f_P \cdot n_e \cdot f_l \cdot f_i \cdot f_c \cdot L
$$

The variables stand, in order, for:

1. the average annual rate of star formation in our galaxy;
2. the fraction of stars with planetary systems;
3. the average number of planets per star within the habitable zone;
4. the fraction of planets on which life develops;
5. the fraction of planets with intelligent life;
6. the fraction of planets whose inhabitants are interested in interstellar communication;
7. the lifetime, in years, of a technological civilization.

The problem is that every value assigned to the last four variables is purely speculative. In particular, it is difficult to estimate the fraction of planets with life without knowing how life originates. If we assume that God creates life directly, this is a free act for which no probability can in principle be calculated. If God employs natural causes in creating life, a probability might in principle be estimated.

_Abiogenesis_ is the natural emergence of life from non-living matter. How that might occur remains an unsolved scientific problem. Without understanding the process, it is difficult to determine its probability.

We do, however, have evidence that abiogenesis is at least not improbable: our own existence. The point seems intuitive. Under which conditions is our existence more probable—if abiogenesis is easy and happens frequently, or if it is difficult and rare? Our existence is naturally more probable if abiogenesis occurs frequently.

The validity of this argument was challenged by the _Carter argument_, which for a long time was widely regarded as successful. In my view, Daniel P. Whitmire decisively refuted it in 2022.

The Carter argument uses Bayes’ theorem, which follows from the axioms of conditional probability. Beyond mathematics, the theorem is used in philosophy and historiography to estimate the probability of hypotheses in light of evidence:

$$
P(H|E)= \frac{P(E|H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E|H) \cdot P(H) + P(E|\neg H) \cdot P(\neg H)}
$$

Here, (P(H|E)) is the probability of hypothesis (H) given evidence (E). Carter’s argument can be stated as follows:

$$
P(AB_{easy}|LoE)= \frac{P(LoE|AB_{easy}) \cdot P(AB_{easy})}{P(LoE|AB_{easy}) \cdot P(AB_{easy}) + P(LoE|AB_{hard}) \cdot P(AB_{hard})}
$$

(LoE) is the evidence that life exists on Earth; (AB_{easy}) is the hypothesis that abiogenesis is easy, and (AB_{hard}) its complement. Carter argues that life on Earth is already a fact regardless of whether abiogenesis is easy or difficult. Therefore:

$$
P(LoE|AB_{easy}) = P(LoE|AB_{hard}) = 1
$$

Since (AB_{hard}) is the complement of (AB_{easy}), their probabilities sum to one. It follows that:

$$
P(AB_{easy}|LoE)= \frac{P(AB_{easy})}{P(AB_{easy}) + P(AB_{hard})} = P(AB_{easy})
$$

On this reasoning, the hypothesis that abiogenesis is easy has the same probability whether or not life exists on Earth. Life on Earth would therefore not be evidence that abiogenesis is easy.

Whitmire identifies the error as an instance of the _old evidence problem_. The mistake is to set

$$
P(LoE|AB_{easy}) = P(LoE|AB_{hard}) = 1
$$

merely because (LoE) is now an established fact. Instead we should consider the hypothetical conditionals

$$
P(LoE\text{ prior to observing }LoE|AB_{easy})
$$

and

$$
P(LoE\text{ prior to observing }LoE|AB_{hard}).
$$

In other words, we ask how probable life on Earth would have been under each hypothesis in the hypothetical situation in which we had not yet observed it.

Nick Bostrom makes the flaw more obvious with a parody argument. Imagine two urns. One contains ten numbered balls; the other contains one million. Ball number 7 is drawn from urn X. What is the probability that X is the urn containing only ten balls? Carter’s reasoning would make (P(X\text{ is the small urn}|\#7)) equal to its prior probability, 0.5, which is clearly wrong.

Using hypothetical conditionals, Whitmire arrives at a value close to one for (P(AB_{easy}|LoE)). The existence of life on Earth is therefore very strong evidence that abiogenesis is easy, although “easy” cannot be quantified more precisely here.

The central problem with the Drake equation nevertheless remains. We cannot give a precise probability that life arises naturally, so we cannot determine whether another civilization exists. We can still say that our own existence is evidence for extraterrestrial life: the easier it is for life to arise, the better this explains the fact that life exists. Conversely, the more difficult it is for life to arise, the better that difficulty explains why we do not observe life arising elsewhere.

## UFO Sightings and Encounters

Apart from probabilistic arguments such as the Drake equation, the only arguments commonly offered for the existence of aliens are alleged sightings and encounters with aliens or UFOs. If genuine, these would contradict the second half of the Fermi paradox: the claim that we have not observed extraterrestrial life.

The Catholic apologist Jimmy Akin, who works for the American apostolate _Catholic Answers_, hosts the podcast _Jimmy Akin’s Mysterious World_. He examines mysteries—ghosts, Loch Ness, and alleged alien encounters among them—from both scientific and Catholic theological perspectives. At the end of each episode Akin weighs competing hypotheses and identifies what he considers the best explanation. ## Compatibility of Aliens and Catholicism

In what follows, _aliens_ means intelligent extraterrestrial life. In Aristotelian-Thomistic terms, human beings are creatures with rational souls. Aliens, although not biologically human, would therefore be persons in the philosophical sense.^[This brackets the possibility of philosophical zombies.] To prevent confusion, we can further qualify the biological definition: humans are rational creatures belonging to the species _Homo sapiens sapiens_.

If aliens exist, God would have created rational life in addition to human beings. This is not in itself novel: angels, too, are creatures with a rational nature. A third kind of rational creature alongside humans and angels is therefore not implausible.

The fact that aliens are not mentioned in Scripture or Tradition is _prima facie_ evidence against their existence, but an argument from silence is generally weak.^[[On arguments from silence](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12136-013-0205-5).] Neither the Church nor Scripture rules out extraterrestrial life. If alien life is scientifically plausible and compatible with Catholic doctrine, we can consider the questions that would arise upon discovering it.

## What Religion Would Aliens Follow?

The answer is necessarily speculative. Even if we cannot assign probabilities, we can list the possibilities. The fact that human societies independently develop concepts of God and life after death gives us some reason to expect similar questions among aliens. Their broad options would include:

1. atheism;
2. agnosticism;
3. monotheism;
4. polytheism;
5. pantheism;
6. deism;
7. autotheism.

Like Aristotle, they might recognize God through rational reflection on creation even without revelation. The rules of inference used in Aristotle’s argument are necessary truths on another planet just as they are on ours, and the empirical premises remain observable there: time passes there too.

St. Paul supports this idea in Romans 1:19–21:

> For what can be known about God is evident to them, because God made it evident to them. Ever since the creation of the world, his invisible reality, his eternal power and divinity, have been perceived with the reason in the works of creation. Therefore they are without excuse.

If God had revealed himself to an extraterrestrial species, its population would presumably also divide between those who follow him and those who reject him. The same kinds of arguments apply here that early medieval philosophers used, before the discovery of the Americas, when speculating about unknown peoples and their relation to God.
